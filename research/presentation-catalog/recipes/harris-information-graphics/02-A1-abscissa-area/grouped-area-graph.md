## Description

In grouped area graphs, the data graphics representing each of the multiple data series are placed one behind the other. With grouped area graphs, the values on the vertical axis for all data series are referenced from a common zero baseline axis, generally the horizontal axis. As a result, the values for each data series can be read directly from the graph, as with grouped line graphs.

Grouped area graphs sometimes cause confusion because there is no easy way to differentiate between a grouped and stacked area graph. In addition, many people think of area graphs as always being stacked. In grouped area graphs, the shaded areas between curves represent differences, not actual values of the data series.

## Handling Intersecting Data Series

When data series intersect on a grouped area graph, there is no sequence of data graphics that will assure full visibility of the curves of all data series. The PDF (p. 12) shows several techniques for addressing this:

1. **Dashed or dotted lines** - Used to indicate hidden curves
2. **Shading or coloring techniques** - Can indicate hidden curves and areas
3. **Separate graphs** - One of the most straightforward methods is to make a separate graph for each data series, which adds clarity but makes comparison more difficult
4. **Three-dimensional grouped area graphs** - Can sometimes make each data series more visible, though they make it harder to quantify values directly

## Reducing Confusion

Grouped area graphs sometimes cause confusion because viewers cannot determine whether the areas extend down to the zero axis. Two methods help overcome this problem (p. 12):

1. **Shift each data graphic slightly** - Expose the edges to show all extend to zero
2. **Use slightly different widths** - Let viewers see that all data graphics extend down

## Scale and Data Variations

The PDF (p. 13) shows five major variations of scales and data used with grouped area graphs:

| Data Pattern | Category Scale | Sequence Scale | Quantitative Scale |
|--------------|---------------|----------------|-------------------|
| All series use common values on horizontal axis | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| All data points have unique values on both axes (paired area graph) | Not applicable | Yes | Yes |

## Negative Values

Grouped area graphs can handle negative values somewhat better than stacked area graphs, but they still have the problem of all or portions of data curves being hidden by the data series towards the front. Both two- and three-dimensional graphs can display negative values.

## Usage

Grouped area graphs are appropriate when:
- You need to read actual values for each data series directly
- The data series need to be compared against a common baseline
- The series do not intersect frequently

Grouped area graphs are less appropriate when:
- Series frequently intersect (visibility problems)
- Viewers are likely to assume stacked interpretation
- The total of all series is important (use stacked instead)

## Notes

Grouped area graphs are used much less frequently than stacked area graphs and can cause misinterpretation since many people automatically associate the stacked variation with area graphs. Consider whether a grouped line graph might serve the same purpose with less potential for confusion.
