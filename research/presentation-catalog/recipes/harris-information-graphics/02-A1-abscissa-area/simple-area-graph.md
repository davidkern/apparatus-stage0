## Description

A simple area graph is a line graph with the area filled between the data curve and the horizontal axis. It is generated by filling this area with shading or color, converting what would be a simple line graph into an area graph. Area graphs are generally not used to convey specific values. Instead, they are most frequently used to show trends and relationships, to identify and/or add emphasis to specific information by virtue of the boldness of the shading or color, or to show parts-of-the-whole.

## Curve Types

Three basic types of curves can be used to generate area graphs:

1. **Segmented curve** - Straight lines connect data points directly
2. **Stepped curve** - Horizontal and vertical segments form a staircase pattern
3. **Smooth curve** - A curved line passes through or near data points

An area graph is sometimes referred to by the type of line used to generate it. For example, an area graph generated by a stepped curve might be called a stepped area graph.

## Examples

> "A simple area graph typically has a quantitative scale on the vertical axis and a category, quantitative, or sequence scale on the horizontal axis." (p. 10)

The PDF shows examples on page 10 with:
- Category scale: data points located directly above every label (Blue, Red, White, Tan, Gray)
- Sequence scale: data points positioned along a time axis ('95, '96)
- Quantitative scale: data points at numeric positions (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14)

Positive and negative values can be plotted on all quantitative scales, as demonstrated in examples on page 10 showing areas that extend both above and below zero.

## Usage

Simple area graphs are appropriate when:
- Showing the trend of a single data series over time or across categories
- Adding visual emphasis to make data more prominent
- The precise values are less important than the overall pattern
- You want to convey the magnitude of cumulative values

Simple area graphs are less appropriate when:
- Precise value reading is required
- Multiple data series need comparison (consider grouped or stacked variants)
- The data has many fluctuations that create a confusing visual pattern

**Scale considerations:**
- With category scales, a data point is located directly above every label, and all categories must be shown
- With sequence and quantitative scales, data points may or may not align with labels
- Many sequence and quantitative graphs can be interpreted even if only beginning and ending values are labeled

## Notes

The line and/or symbols representing the data points may or may not be shown on the final graph. Including plot symbols helps viewers identify where actual data points were recorded versus interpolated regions.
