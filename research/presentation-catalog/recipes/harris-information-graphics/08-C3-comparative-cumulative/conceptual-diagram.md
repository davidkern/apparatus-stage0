## Description

A conceptual diagram provides a graphical overview of how people, things, ideas, influences, actions, and other elements interrelate. These diagrams condense large amounts of information into a single chart and are particularly effective for presenting material that is still in the idea stage. They work equally well for communicating both simple and complex concepts.

Conceptual diagrams are typically non-quantitative and generally do not go into much detail. They are applicable to any subject, whether concrete or abstract, theoretical or actual, current, historical, or future.

The distinction between conceptual diagrams and other diagram types (such as flow charts and organizational charts) is not always clear-cut. The key differentiator is the level of detail: if a diagram becomes fairly detailed, it generally is not considered a conceptual diagram. Some block diagrams and Venn diagrams are classified as conceptual diagrams.

## Examples

Harris illustrates four conceptual diagrams on page 100:

> A conceptual diagram indicating the relationship between a key decision maker, influencing factors (work force, personal goals, community, media, economy, stockholders), and some of the constraints (policies and procedures) that the entire system operates within.

> A conceptual diagram indicating how the future of product lines might relate to current market evaluations, using a matrix with market growth rate vs. market share to categorize products into "Invest & grow," "Grow?," "Divest?," and "Divest" quadrants.

> A conceptual diagram indicating the interrelationship of authority and responsibility among various departments within an organization (Marketing, Finance, Operations shown as overlapping circles).

> A conceptual diagram showing how investors, companies, and markets interrelate through a cyclical flow of investment, company building, sales, and profit distribution.

## Usage

Conceptual diagrams are particularly effective in presentations because:
- They can be rapidly understood
- They tend to have significant impact on the audience
- They may be remembered longer than the same subject presented using words and numbers alone

When using arrows in conceptual diagrams, be aware that arrow meanings can vary significantly:
- Call attention to something
- Indicate direction
- Depict flow from one thing to another
- Show influence or impact of one thing on another
- Bidirectional arrows may indicate mutual, interdependent influences
- Indicate an action to be taken (as opposed to no action)
- Show that something shifts or changes (e.g., asset shifting to liability) even though nothing physically moves

Despite the varied potential meanings of symbols, legends are seldom used. Well-designed conceptual diagrams should be self-explanatory, though text explanations and discussions normally accompany them.

## Notes

Conceptual diagrams represent a meta-category that encompasses various specific diagram types when used at a high level of abstraction. The technique prioritizes clarity of relationships over quantitative precision or procedural detail.
