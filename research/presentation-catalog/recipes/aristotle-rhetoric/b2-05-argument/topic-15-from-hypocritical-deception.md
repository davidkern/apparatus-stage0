## Description

The fifteenth common topic exposes the gap between what people praise openly (phanerōs) and what they praise secretly (aphanōs). Publicly, people praise the just and the beautiful; privately, they wish for what is advantageous to themselves.

Aristotle calls this "the most effective topic in dealing with paradoxes" - arguments that seem to contradict common opinion. By appealing to what people really want (rather than what they say they want), the speaker can draw conclusions that align with hidden preferences.

## Examples

> "Another: when [one's opponents] do not praise the same things openly as they do secretly, but to a great extent openly praise the just and beautiful while privately they wish rather for what is to their advantage, try to draw the other conclusion from what they say." (1399a30-1399b1)

The structure:
1. Observe what opponents publicly praise (justice, beauty)
2. Identify what they privately desire (advantage)
3. Draw conclusions from their private wish, not their public stance

> "For this is the most effective topic in dealing with paradoxes." (1399b1-2)

Aristotle emphasizes the topic's power: it cuts through public posturing to appeal to actual motivations.

## Usage

This topic operates in two modes:

**Exposing hypocrisy:**
- Show that the opponent's public stance contradicts their private interests
- Their private behavior reveals what they really believe
- Therefore their public arguments are insincere

**Exploiting private motive:**
- When making a paradoxical argument (one that contradicts conventional opinion)
- Appeal not to what people say they want, but to what they actually want
- This can make unconventional conclusions persuasive

The topic is particularly useful when:
- Opponents have taken a publicly virtuous position
- You can demonstrate their actual preferences differ
- The audience is sophisticated enough to recognize the gap

## Notes

This topic reveals Aristotle's realist psychology: people often have divergent public and private preferences. The skilled speaker identifies this gap and exploits it.

The reference to "paradoxes" connects to the sophistic tradition of arguing for surprising or counter-intuitive conclusions. This topic provides a method: bypass stated preferences and appeal to actual ones.
