## Description

The thirteenth common topic argues from consequences (to akolouthoun - "what follows"). The speaker examines what good or bad results follow from something, using these consequences to exhort, dissuade, praise, blame, accuse, or defend.

This topic applies across all three species of rhetoric:
- Deliberative: Exhort toward good consequences, dissuade from bad
- Epideictic: Praise for good consequences, blame for bad
- Judicial: Accuse based on harmful consequences, defend based on beneficial ones

Aristotle notes that "this topic constitutes the Art of Callippus, with the addition of the possible and other things that have been mentioned."

## Examples

> "Another is to exhort or dissuade and accuse or defend and praise or blame on the basis of the consequence, since in most instances it happens that something good and bad follows from the same [cause]." (1399a13-17)

Aristotle establishes the scope: consequences can be used for any rhetorical purpose.

> "For example, being envied is an evil result of being educated, but the wisdom [acquired] is a good thing; therefore, [it may be argued,] one should not be educated; for one ought not be envied." (1399a17-20)

An argument against education based on its bad consequence (envy).

> "On the other hand, one should be educated; for one ought to be wise." (1399a20-21)

The counter-argument based on education's good consequence (wisdom).

The same action (getting educated) has both good consequences (wisdom) and bad consequences (envy). The speaker selects which to emphasize based on their purpose.

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- You need to evaluate a course of action or a person's character
- Consequences are known or can be established
- The audience will be moved by the consequences cited

Key insight: Most things have mixed consequences. Rhetorical skill lies in:
1. Selecting which consequences to emphasize
2. Framing consequences effectively
3. Anticipating counter-arguments based on opposite consequences

The education example shows the technique's bidirectionality: the same topic generates arguments on both sides. A skilled speaker can use consequences for exhortation or dissuasion as needed.

## Notes

Kennedy identifies Callippus as "a student of Isocrates and author of a handbook." The reference to "the possible and other things" links to the discussion of special topics in 2.19 about possibility, past fact, future fact, and magnitude.

The topic's prominence - Aristotle says it forms an entire techne (art) - reflects the centrality of consequentialist reasoning in practical rhetoric.
