## Description

The twenty-seventh common topic argues from what would have been a mistake (ek tōn hamartēthentōn). The logic examines incomplete patterns of action: if someone did X but didn't do Y (which would have been necessary to complete the pattern), then either they didn't do X, or their not doing Y was a mistake that reveals something.

Aristotle notes this topic "and species of enthymeme is the whole art before Theodorus."

## Examples

> "Another topic is to accuse or defend on the basis of what would have been a mistake." (1401a12-13)

Aristotle states the topic's dual application.

> "For example, in the Medea of Carcinus some accuse her on the ground that she has killed her children. At any rate, they are not to be seen; for Medea made the mistake of sending the children away." (1401a13-17)

The accusation:
- Evidence: The children are not to be seen
- Inference: Medea killed them
- Prosecutor's point: Sending the children away was a "mistake" (suspicious action that she shouldn't have done if innocent)

> "But she defends herself on the ground that [it is improbable she has killed them, because] she would have killed Jason [then as well], not [only] the children; for she would have made a mistake in not doing so if she had done the other thing." (1401a17-21)

The defense:
- If Medea had killed the children → she should have killed Jason too
- She didn't kill Jason → therefore she probably didn't kill the children
- Her "not killing Jason" would have been a mistake IF she killed the children
- Since the incomplete action doesn't make sense, the complete action probably didn't happen

The defense argues from the incompleteness of the alleged crime: a rational Medea who killed her children wouldn't have spared Jason.

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- An incomplete pattern of action exists
- The incompleteness suggests the action didn't occur (defense)
- OR the incompleteness was a revealing mistake (accusation)

**For accusation:**
- Point to actions that would only make sense if the defendant is guilty
- "You did X, which would be a mistake if you were innocent"

**For defense:**
- Point to actions NOT taken that would have completed the alleged pattern
- "If I did X, I would have done Y too - but I didn't do Y, so I probably didn't do X"

## Notes

Kennedy identifies Theodorus as a "rhetorician of the late fifth century BCE." The claim that this single topic constituted an entire rhetorical art before Theodorus suggests its fundamental importance.

The Medea example is structurally interesting: both accusation and defense use the same topic but point to different "mistakes." The accusation focuses on what she did (sending children away); the defense focuses on what she didn't do (kill Jason).
