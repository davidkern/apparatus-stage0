## Description

The twelfth common topic argues from the parts (merē). The speaker examines the component parts or species within a genus, reasoning from what is true of the parts to what is true of the whole, or vice versa.

Aristotle notes this topic is "discussed in the Topics" where parts means "the species within a defined genus."

## Examples

> "Another [topic] is from the parts, as discussed in the Topics; [for example,] What kind of motion is the soul? For it is this or that." (1399a6-9)

The example invokes Aristotle's philosophical analysis of motion: if the soul is a kind of motion, it must be one of the recognized types (generation, destruction, increase, diminution, alteration, change of place). By examining each type of motion, one can determine what kind of motion the soul is.

> "There is an example from the Socrates of Theodectes: 'Against what holy place has he profaned? Which gods that the city recognizes has he not believed in?'" (1399a9-13)

A defense of Socrates against charges of impiety. The argument examines the parts:
- Which specific holy place was profaned? (None can be named)
- Which specific recognized god was rejected? (None can be named)

By examining each part of the accusation, the defense shows the general charge has no substance.

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- A general claim needs to be tested by examining specifics
- The parts or species of something are known and enumerable
- Examining parts reveals what is true (or not true) of the whole

Two directions:
1. **Parts to whole**: If something is true of all parts, it is true of the whole
2. **Whole to parts**: If something is true of the whole, it must be true of some part

The Socrates example shows defensive application: forcing the accuser to specify which part of the charge applies. If no specific instance can be given, the general charge fails.

## Notes

Kennedy notes the Socrates speech is "probably one of the apologies written by fourth-century BCE authors, of which those by Plato and Xenophon survive. If, as it seems, Aristotle is quoting literally, use of the third person indicates that Theodectes' version took the imagined form of a speech by some advocate."

The relationship between this topic and Topic 9 (from division) is close: both involve breaking a whole into parts. Division emphasizes exhaustive alternatives; Parts emphasizes component analysis.
