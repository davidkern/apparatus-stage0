## Description

The sixth common topic turns what has been said against oneself back upon the one who said it. This is a powerful technique in adversarial rhetoric - using the opponent's own words, accusations, or standards as the basis for an argument against them.

The technique works best when the opponent has lower credibility than the speaker. If the opponent is more likely to have committed the alleged wrong, turning the argument back exposes this. However, Aristotle warns that the technique backfires if the opponent has higher credibility.

## Examples

> "Another is from [turning] what has been said against oneself upon the one who said it, but the way of doing it differs [with the context]." (1398a16-17)

Aristotle notes the technique varies by situation.

> "[There is] the argument Iphicrates used against Aristophon when he asked [the latter] if he would betray the fleet for money. After [Aristophon] denied it, [Iphicrates] said, 'If you, being Aristophon, would not play the traitor, would I, Iphicrates?'" (1398a20-24)

The structure: If even Aristophon (with his reputation) wouldn't betray the fleet, how much less would Iphicrates (with his superior reputation)?

Kennedy notes: "The occasion was probably allegations of treachery against Iphicrates during the Social War of 357-355 BCE. The basis of the argument is the contrasting reputation of the two speakers."

Aristotle adds a crucial caveat:

> "But the opponent should be the one who seems more likely to have done wrong. Otherwise, it would seem ludicrous if someone were to say this in reply[, for example,] to a prosecution by Aristides." (1398a24-27)

Aristides "the Just" was a famously honorable statesman. Turning an accusation back on him would backfire because his credibility exceeds the defendant's.

> "In general, it is out of place when someone reproaches others for [failing to do] what he does not do - or would not do - himself." (1398a28-30)

The technique is inappropriate when the accuser does not himself meet the standard being invoked.

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- The opponent has made an accusation or set a standard
- You can show that the same accusation applies more strongly to them
- OR you can show that your credibility exceeds theirs

The technique serves two purposes:
1. **Discrediting the accuser**: The accuser cannot be morally superior if the accusation applies to them
2. **Exonerating by comparison**: If a less credible person wouldn't do X, a more credible person certainly wouldn't

Critical condition: Your relative ethos must support the reversal. The technique requires that "the accuser always wants to be morally superior to the defendant. This, then, should be disproved."

## Notes

This topic shows how enthymemes can operate through ethos (character/credibility) as well as logos. The argument form is logical (if A, then B), but the content depends on comparative reputation.

The reference to a lost example from Sophocles' Teucer suggests the technique had dramatic as well as forensic applications.
