## Description

The nineteenth common topic argues from purpose or motive (to hou heneka - "that for the sake of which"). The speaker asserts that the purpose for which something might exist or happen is in fact the cause for which it does exist or has happened.

This topic allows attribution of motive based on possible benefit or effect, even without direct evidence of intention.

## Examples

> "Another is to say that the purpose for which something might exist or might happen is the cause for which it does exist or has happened; for example, if someone gave somebody something so that he could cause him pain after he took it away." (1400a1-5)

The example presents a cynical interpretation: a gift was given specifically so that withdrawing it would cause pain. The possible purpose (causing pain) is treated as the actual motive.

> "This is also the source of the following [quotation from some unidentified tragedy]:
> God gives great good fortune to many, not out of good will,
> But so that the disasters people experience may be more obvious." (1400a5-8)

A tragic interpretation of divine beneficence: fortune is given not from kindness but to make eventual suffering more stark. The possible dark purpose is attributed as the actual motive.

> "And this from the Meleager of Antiphon:
> Not that they may kill the beast, but that
> They may be witnesses to Greece of the courage of Meleager." (1400a9-11)

Why were hunters gathered? Not for the hunt itself, but so Meleager's valor could be witnessed. The attributed purpose elevates the protagonist.

> "And from the Ajax of Theodectes, that Diomedes chose Odysseus not out of honor to him but in order that his companion might be inferior; for he could have done it for this reason." (1400a11-15)

Why did Diomedes choose Odysseus as companion? Not to honor him, but to ensure his own superiority by comparison. A cynical motive is attributed.

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- An action has occurred whose motive is unknown or disputed
- A possible purpose can be identified
- You want to attribute that purpose as the actual motive

The topic works in two directions:

**Cynical attribution**: Assigning base motives
- The gift was given to cause pain
- Fortune is given to magnify suffering
- The choice was made from competitive jealousy

**Charitable attribution**: Assigning noble motives
- The hunters were gathered to witness greatness
- The action was taken for the benefit of others

The key phrase is "for he could have done it for this reason" - the mere possibility of a purpose is treated as evidence for that purpose.

## Notes

This topic involves a logical leap: from "X could have purpose P" to "X was done for purpose P." Aristotle presents it without explicit warning, though the examples suggest awareness of its speculative nature.

The tragic examples show how this reasoning pattern pervades Greek literature, particularly in interpreting divine and heroic action.
