## Description

The fifth type of apparent enthymeme argues from accidental results (dia to symbebēkos). When two things happen to correlate, the speaker treats this as a meaningful or essential connection. But the correlation is merely accidental - circumstantial rather than causal or definitional.

## Examples

> "Another is through an accidental result; for example, Polycrates says of mice that they aided [the Egyptians] by gnawing the bowstrings [of the invading Assyrians]." (1401b19-21)

Polycrates (the sophist) argues mice are worthy creatures because they helped Egypt by gnawing enemy bowstrings (a story from Herodotus 2.141).

The problem: mice gnaw things by nature; it was accidental that this gnawing benefited Egypt. The mice didn't intentionally help; they just behaved as mice do.

> "Or if someone were to say that to be invited to dinner is the greatest form of honor; for Achilles' wrath against the Achaeans at Tenedos resulted from not being invited." (1401b21-25)

The argument:
- Achilles was wrathful because he wasn't invited to dinner
- Therefore dinner invitations are the greatest honor

But Achilles wasn't angry ABOUT the dinner - he was angry about being dishonored. Not being invited was merely an incidental expression of the dishonor, not its essence.

> "But he was angry because of being dishonored, and not being invited to dinner was an accidental result of this." (1401b25-27)

Aristotle makes the distinction explicit: the dinner invitation was accidental to the real issue (dishonor). Treating the accident as the cause reverses the actual relationship.

## Usage

**For recognition:**
- Notice when coincidental benefits or harms are treated as intentional
- Ask: "Is this connection essential or merely circumstantial?"
- Check whether the correlation reflects the actual cause

**Common forms:**
- Natural behavior that accidentally produces benefit → treated as intentional aid
- Incidental circumstances of an event → treated as its cause
- Symptoms of a situation → treated as its essence

**Key distinction:**
- Essential connection: X is the reason for Y
- Accidental connection: X happens to accompany Y

## Notes

The Achilles example shows how accidental properties can be mistaken for essential ones. A thorough analysis reveals the underlying cause (dishonor), of which the dinner invitation was merely a symptom or expression.

Polycrates was a sophist known for paradoxical arguments, including an encomium of salt and a mock accusation of Socrates. His mouse-argument exemplifies sophistical exploitation of accidents.
