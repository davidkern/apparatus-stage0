## Description

Objections (enstasis - literally "stepping in") challenge an opponent's enthymeme in four ways:

1. **From the original argument itself**: Turn the opponent's own premises against them
2. **From something similar**: Cite a parallel case where the conclusion doesn't hold
3. **From the opposite**: Show that the opposite premise doesn't yield the opposite conclusion
4. **From previous judgment**: Cite authoritative opinions that contradict the conclusion

These methods are drawn from the Topics but applied rhetorically.

## Examples

> "Objections are brought, as has also been described in the Topics, in four ways; for either they are derived from the original argument or from something similar or from the opposite or from what has been judged true." (1402b17-20)

Aristotle lists the four sources of objection.

### From the original argument:

> "By the original argument I mean, for example, if the enthymeme concerned love, claiming it was good, the objection [then] would be double; for it could be generally claimed that all lack is bad [and love is a feeling of lacking something]." (1402b20-24)

The opponent argues love is good. Objection from the same argument: love is a form of lack (desire for what one doesn't have); all lack is bad; therefore love might be bad.

> "Or, taking one kind, [one could claim] that there would be no talk of Caunian love if there were not also bad forms." (1402b24-26)

Caunian love = incest (from the myth of Caunus). If all love were good, we wouldn't speak of "Caunian love" as something bad. The existence of bad forms of love contradicts the general claim.

### From the opposite:

> "Objection is brought by the opposite if, for example, the enthymeme was that 'a good man benefits all his friends'; [the objection is,] 'But the wicked man does not harm [all his friends].'" (1402b26-29)

If "good men benefit all friends" were simply true, we'd expect the opposite ("wicked men harm all friends") to be true. But wicked men don't harm ALL their friends. The asymmetry undermines the original claim.

### From something similar:

> "[An objection is based] on something similar [to the original argument]; if the enthymeme was that those who suffer always hate [their oppressor], [the objection is,] 'But those who are benefited do not always love [their benefactors].'" (1402b29-32)

If suffering always produces hatred, we'd expect the similar claim (benefit always produces love) to hold. But people don't always love their benefactors. The parallel fails, undermining the original.

### From previous judgment:

> "'Judgments' are those of well-known men; for example, if someone spoke the enthymeme that one should excuse those who are drunk, for their mistakes are made out of ignorance. The objection is that then Pittacus didn't know what he was doing; for he legislated greater punishments if someone committed a misdeed when drunk." (1402b32-1403a1)

The opponent argues drunkenness excuses wrongdoing (due to ignorance). Objection: Pittacus, a respected lawgiver, imposed GREATER penalties for drunken offenses - an authoritative judgment that contradicts the excuse.

## Usage

Choose the objection type based on available resources:

1. **From original**: Look for internal contradictions or implications the opponent didn't consider
2. **From similar**: Find parallel cases where the claimed pattern fails
3. **From opposite**: Test whether the opposite claim has the expected opposite consequences
4. **From judgment**: Cite respected authorities who contradict the opponent's position

Each type has different rhetorical force:
- Original and opposite show logical problems
- Similar shows empirical counterexamples
- Judgment adds authoritative weight

## Notes

Aristotle refers to "the Topics" (his dialectical treatise) as the source for this classification. The rhetorical application focuses on what works persuasively with audiences, not just what is logically valid.

Pittacus was one of the Seven Sages of Greece and a lawgiver at Mytilene in the early 6th century BCE. His judgment against drunkenness-as-excuse carried great authority.
