## Description

The tenth common topic argues from induction (epagōgē). The speaker presents particular instances and reasons from them to a general principle, which is then applied to the case at hand.

This is the enthymematic form of what Aristotle elsewhere calls paradigm (paradeigma) - rhetorical induction. The examples establish a pattern that the audience generalizes.

## Examples

> "In the case of the woman of Peparethus [it was argued] that women everywhere discern the truth about [the father of] children; for when the orator Mantias at Athens was disputing [the parentage of] his son, the boy's mother declared the truth. Similarly, when Ismenias and Stilbon were in a dispute at Thebes, the woman of Dodona identified the son of Ismenias; and for this reason Thettaliscus was recognized as Ismenias' son." (1398b10-19)

Multiple instances of mothers correctly identifying paternity support the general claim that women discern the truth about fatherhood. This general principle is then applied to the specific case at hand.

> "[Another example is] from the Law speech of Theodectes [to the effect that] if people do not entrust their horses to those who take poor care of others' horses nor ships to those who have overturned others' ships - if then this is similarly true in all cases - one should not employ for one's own safety those who have poorly guarded the safety of another." (1398b19-24)

Two parallel instances (horses, ships) establish a general principle about not trusting those with poor track records.

> "[Another example is] as Alcidamas [argued], that all honor the wise; at least, Parians honored Archilochus despite the nasty things he said [about them]; and Chians Homer, though he was not a citizen; and Mytilenaeans Sappho, although a woman; and Lacedaimonians, though least fond of literature, made Chilon a member of their council of elders, and the Italiotes honored Pythagoras and the Lampsacenes buried Anaxagoras, though a foreigner, and even now still honor him." (1398b24-29)

A rich accumulation of instances: Archilochus, Homer, Sappho, Chilon, Pythagoras, Anaxagoras - each honored despite obstacles. The accumulation establishes: "all honor the wise."

> "And Athenians were prosperous while using the laws of Solon, and Lacedaimonians when [using] those of Lycurgus; and at Thebes, at the time the leaders became philosophers, the city prospered." (1398b29-30)

Three instances of prosperity under wise leadership support a general claim about the value of philosophical governance.

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- You have multiple instances that support your general claim
- The instances are well-known or easily established
- The pattern is genuinely representative

The strength of inductive arguments depends on:
1. **Number of instances**: More examples strengthen the generalization
2. **Quality of instances**: Well-known, respected examples carry more weight
3. **Variety of instances**: Examples from different contexts suggest the pattern is universal
4. **Absence of counterexamples**: One clear counterexample defeats the induction

The Alcidamas example shows effective technique: multiple instances from different cities, different circumstances, even despite obstacles - all pointing to the same conclusion.

## Notes

Induction (epagōgē) in rhetoric relates to but differs from scientific induction. Rhetorical induction need not be exhaustive or demonstrative - it must be persuasive. The accumulation of instances creates psychological force even when logical certainty is absent.

Kennedy notes the final sentence about Thebes may be a later addition, referring to Epaminondas and Pelopidas (371-361 BCE).
