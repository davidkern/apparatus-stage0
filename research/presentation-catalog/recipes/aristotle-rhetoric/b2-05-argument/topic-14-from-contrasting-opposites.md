## Description

The fourteenth common topic contrasts two opposed matters (duoin antikeimenōin). Unlike Topic 13, which examines consequences of a single action, this topic sets up two genuinely opposite courses of action and shows that both lead to favorable (or unfavorable) outcomes.

Aristotle calls this structure "blaisōsis" (a twist or dilemma). The power lies in showing the audience they cannot escape a conclusion regardless of which alternative they choose.

## Examples

> "Another: when there is need to exhort or dissuade on two matters that are contrasted, [one needs] to use the method mentioned in both; but there is the difference that in the previous case any two things are contrasted, while here they are opposites." (1399a21-24)

Aristotle distinguishes this from Topic 13: here the alternatives are genuine opposites, not just any two things.

> "For example, a priestess did not allow her son to engage in public debate: 'For,' she said, 'if you say what is just, the people will hate you, but if what is unjust, the gods will.'" (1399a24-27)

A dissuasive dilemma:
- If you speak justly → the people hate you (bad)
- If you speak unjustly → the gods hate you (bad)
- Either way → bad outcome
- Conclusion: don't engage in public debate

> "On the other hand, you should engage in public debate; for if you speak what is just, the gods will love you, if what is unjust, the people will." (1399a27-29)

The counter-dilemma reverses the valence:
- If you speak justly → the gods love you (good)
- If you speak unjustly → the people love you (good)
- Either way → good outcome
- Conclusion: engage in public debate

> "This is the same as what is said about buying the marsh with the salt." (1399a29-30)

A proverb meaning "taking the bad with the good" - both outcomes accompany the choice.

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- Two genuine opposites are available as alternatives
- You can show both lead to favorable (for exhortation) or unfavorable (for dissuasion) outcomes
- You want to create a sense of inevitability

The dilemma structure is powerful but reversible. The priestess's dissuasive dilemma can be turned into an exhortative one by reframing the consequences. Speakers should anticipate this counter-move.

Aristotle notes: "This dilemma [blaisōsis, 'twist'] occurs whenever good and evil follow either of two things, each opposite to each."

## Notes

The term blaisōsis (twist) captures the logical trap: the audience is "twisted" toward the conclusion regardless of their initial preference. This is a more elaborate form of consequentialist argument than Topic 13, requiring the speaker to analyze both alternatives fully.

The example also shows bidirectionality: the same structural setup can argue for opposite conclusions depending on how consequences are framed.
