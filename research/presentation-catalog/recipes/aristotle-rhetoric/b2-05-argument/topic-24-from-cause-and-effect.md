## Description

The twenty-fourth common topic argues from cause (apo tou aitiou). The structure is straightforward: if the cause exists, so does the effect; if the cause does not exist, neither does the effect. "The cause and that of which it is the cause go together, and without cause there is nothing."

This topic allows both establishing that something happened (by showing the cause existed) and denying that something happened (by showing the cause was absent).

## Examples

> "Another is from the cause [and effect]: if the cause exists, so does the effect; if it does not, there is no effect. The cause and that of which it is the cause go together, and without cause there is nothing." (1400b21-24)

Aristotle states the principle directly.

> "For example, when Leodamas was defending himself against Thrasybulus' charge that his name had been cut out [from some inscription] in the time of the Thirty [Tyrants], he said it was not possible; for the Thirty would have trusted him more if his hatred of the democracy had remained inscribed." (1400b24-29)

The argument:
- Alleged effect: Leodamas's name was erased from an inscription
- Alleged implication: He was anti-democratic (and tried to hide it)
- Leodamas's response: The erasure couldn't have happened
- His causal reasoning: The Thirty (who were anti-democratic) would have trusted him MORE with the inscription intact
- Therefore: He had no reason to erase it; the alleged cause (desire to hide anti-democratic sentiment) was actually a reason NOT to erase

The defense works by showing the alleged cause would have produced the opposite effect.

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- A causal relationship is alleged
- You want to establish or deny that something happened
- The presence or absence of the cause can be demonstrated

Two primary applications:

**Establish effect from cause:**
- Show the cause was present
- Conclude the effect must have occurred

**Deny effect from absent cause:**
- Show the cause was absent
- Conclude the effect could not have occurred

**Deny effect by showing counter-motive (as in the example):**
- Show that what's alleged as the cause would actually produce the opposite effect
- Therefore the alleged effect didn't happen

## Notes

The Leodamas example is particularly sophisticated: rather than simply denying the erasure, he shows that the alleged motive (hiding anti-democratic sentiment) would have been served better by NOT erasing the inscription. This turns the causal argument against the accuser.

The topic relates to but differs from the fallacious post hoc reasoning discussed in Chapter 24: legitimate causal argument requires genuine causal connection, not mere temporal sequence.
