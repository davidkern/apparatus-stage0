## Description

The fifth common topic looks at time (ton khronon skopein) to construct arguments. The core move is: if something would have been granted before an achievement, it certainly should be granted after; if something was promised in anticipation, it should be delivered in realization.

Kennedy notes this becomes known as a "plastos" or "fictive" argument - imagining what would have been agreed to at a different time and using that to establish what should be agreed to now.

This is a temporal variant of a fortiori reasoning: the later time provides equal or stronger grounds for the conclusion.

## Examples

> "What Iphicrates said in the [suit] against Harmodius: 'If, before accomplishing anything, I asked to be honored with a statue if I succeeded, you would have granted it. Will you not grant it [now] that I have succeeded? Do not then make a promise in anticipation but refuse it in realization.'" (1398a5-10)

Iphicrates argues: if you would have promised the honor beforehand (when I had only the potential), you must grant it now (when I have the achievement). The earlier hypothetical commitment binds the later actual situation.

Kennedy notes: "In 390 BCE a statue was voted in honor of the general Iphicrates for his services to Athens. When after twenty years it had not been erected he brought successful action to implement the grant."

> "On the subject of [allowing] Philip to pass through Thebes into Attica [the Macedonian ambassador said] that if [the request had been made] before [Philip] decided to help [the Thebans by moving] into Phocis, [the Thebans] would have promised [to let him pass through their territory into Attica]; it is strange, then, if they will not let him pass [now] because he let that opportunity go and trusted them." (1398a10-15)

A diplomatic application: Philip trusted the Thebans instead of demanding passage as a condition. The earlier hypothetical agreement should bind them now - refusing passage after he showed trust would be worse than if he had demanded it as a precondition.

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- A commitment was made (or would have been made) at an earlier time
- The current situation provides equal or better grounds for that commitment
- Refusing now would be inconsistent with the earlier position

The structure often involves a hypothetical: "If you would have agreed before X, you must agree now that X has occurred."

The topic is particularly powerful in:
- **Claims for honors/rewards**: The Iphicrates example
- **Diplomatic negotiations**: The Philip example
- **Contract disputes**: Where conditions have been fulfilled

## Notes

This topic is related to the more-and-less topic (4) but specifically exploits temporal comparison. The passage of time often creates stronger grounds (achievement vs. promise) while maintaining the logical continuity of commitment.

Kennedy notes this is "one of the last datable references in the Rhetoric" - the Philip example from 346-339 BCE suggests late additions to the text.
