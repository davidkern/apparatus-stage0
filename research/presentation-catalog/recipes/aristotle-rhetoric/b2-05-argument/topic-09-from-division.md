## Description

The ninth common topic argues from division (dihairesis). The speaker divides all possibilities into an exhaustive set, eliminates all but one, and concludes that the remaining possibility must be true.

This is a form of disjunctive syllogism: Either A, B, or C; not A and not B; therefore C.

The power of the technique lies in its apparent logical completeness. If the division is genuinely exhaustive and the eliminations are valid, the conclusion is necessary.

## Examples

> "Another is from division; for example, if [one says,] 'All people do wrong for one of three reasons: either for this, or this, or this; now two of these are impossible, but even [the accusers] themselves do not assert the third.'" (1398b7-10)

The structure:
1. Division: All wrongdoing has three possible motives (the specific motives are not given in this abbreviated example)
2. Elimination: Two motives are impossible in this case
3. Further elimination: The accusers don't even claim the third applies
4. Conclusion: The defendant did not do wrong (no motive applies)

This is a defense strategy in judicial rhetoric - showing that none of the possible motives for wrongdoing apply to the defendant.

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- The possibilities can be exhaustively enumerated
- You can eliminate all alternatives except your desired conclusion
- The division is credible to the audience

The technique is particularly effective for:
- **Defense**: Showing no motive for alleged wrongdoing
- **Deliberation**: Showing only one course of action is viable
- **Explanation**: Identifying a cause by process of elimination

Critical requirements:
1. **Exhaustiveness**: The division must cover all possibilities. If a fourth option exists, the argument collapses.
2. **Valid elimination**: Each alternative must be genuinely ruled out, not merely asserted to be impossible.

## Notes

Aristotle notes: "The use of logical divisions in rhetoric is recommended in Plato's Phaedrus 265e-266a and is the method followed in Gorgias' Helen."

The technique connects rhetorical argument to philosophical method (dihairesis is central to Platonic dialectic). In rhetoric, the division need not be philosophically rigorous - it need only be persuasive to the audience.
