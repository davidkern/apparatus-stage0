## Description

The twenty-fifth common topic examines whether a better plan existed (ek beltion allōs - "from a better alternative"). If someone could have acted more effectively but didn't, this can be evidence they didn't act at all (because rational actors choose the best available means).

Conversely, if no better alternative existed, a policy or action is defended by showing it was the best available option.

Aristotle warns this reasoning "may be false" because better alternatives often become clear only in hindsight.

## Examples

> "Another is to see if there was or is a better plan of a different sort from what is advised or is being done or has been done; for it is evident that if this is so, it has not been acted on; for no one willingly and knowingly chooses the bad." (1400b29-1401a2)

The basic principle: rational agents choose the best available means. If a better alternative existed but wasn't taken, perhaps the alleged action wasn't taken at all.

Aristotle immediately adds the caveat:

> "But this may be false reasoning; for it often later becomes clear how things could have been better done but earlier it was unclear." (1401a2-4)

The qualification: hindsight is not foresight. What seems obviously better now may not have been apparent at the time of action.

## Usage

The topic has two applications:

**Defensive (primary use):**
- If accused of wrongdoing, show that a more effective means existed
- Argue: "If I wanted X, I would have done Y instead - it would have been more effective"
- Therefore: I didn't want X, so I'm not guilty

**Policy defense (the inverse):**
- Show that no better alternative existed at the time
- Argue: "This was the best available option"
- Kennedy notes this is "the major argument in Demosthenes' speech On the Crown in defense of his policy against Macedon: there was no better plan"

Use this topic when:
- Accused of an action that had an obviously better alternative
- Defending a policy as the best available option
- The rationality of the actor is a premise

Critical limitation: The argument depends on what was knowable at the time. Better alternatives visible in hindsight do not undermine choices made with limited information.

## Notes

This topic embodies a principle of rational action theory: agents choose what they believe to be best. But Aristotle's warning shows his awareness that belief about what's best depends on available information.

The reference to Demosthenes' On the Crown connects the abstract topic to famous rhetorical practice: defending a policy by arguing no better course existed.
