## Description

The sixth type of apparent enthymeme reasons from what follows (para to hepomenon) - affirming the consequent. The logical error: from "if X then Y" and "Y," one cannot conclude "X." The conditional is not convertible.

The fallacy occurs when someone has a property that the high-minded (or guilty, or happy) ALSO have, and they're therefore judged to be high-minded (or guilty, or happy). But many people can have the same property for different reasons.

## Examples

> "Another is in terms of what follows [from a fallacious assumption], for example, in the Alexander [the claim] that [Paris] was 'high-minded'; for looking down on the society of the multitude he passed his time by himself on Mount Ida." (1401b29-33)

The argument:
- The high-minded look down on crowds and live in solitude
- Paris looked down on crowds and lived on Mount Ida
- Therefore Paris is high-minded

The fallacy: Solitary living follows from high-mindedness, but it doesn't PROVE high-mindedness. Paris could have lived alone for other reasons.

> "[The argument is] that because the high-minded have this quality, he, too, should be thought high-minded." (1401b33-35)

Aristotle identifies the structure: possessing a quality of the high-minded doesn't make one high-minded.

> "And when someone is a dandy and roams the streets at night, he is sexually promiscuous; for that is the way such people act." (1401b35-1402a1)

Promiscuous people may dress well and roam at night, but dressing well and roaming at night doesn't prove promiscuity.

> "Similarly, too, because beggars sing and dance in temple precincts and because exiles can live wherever they want, since these things are true of those seeming to be happy, such people might seem to be happy." (1402a1-4)

- Happy people can sing, dance, and live anywhere
- Beggars sing and dance; exiles can live anywhere
- Therefore beggars and exiles are happy?

The consequent (freedom to sing/move) doesn't prove the antecedent (happiness).

> "But there is a difference in the circumstances. Thus, it, too, falls under the [fallacy of] omission." (1402a4-5)

Aristotle notes that circumstances differ - this is also a fallacy of omission, failing to note the different conditions under which the behavior occurs.

## Usage

**For recognition:**
- Notice when observed behavior is used to infer internal states or character
- Check whether the inference runs the wrong direction (consequent to antecedent)
- Ask: "Could this behavior have a different explanation?"

**Logical structure:**
- Valid: If high-minded, then solitary (If X, then Y)
- Invalid: Solitary, therefore high-minded (Y, therefore X)

**Common domains:**
- Character inference from behavior
- Guilt inference from circumstances
- Status inference from appearances

## Notes

This is one of the most common informal fallacies. In formal logic it's called "affirming the consequent." Aristotle's examples show it pervades everyday reasoning about character, guilt, and status.

Kennedy notes: "Paris scorned the crowd; for he lived in the mountains. The high-minded scorn the crowd. Therefore, as a consequence, Paris is high-minded. But scorning the crowd is not convertible with being high-minded."
