## Description

The twenty-eighth and final common topic argues from a name (apo tou onomatos). The speaker derives conclusions from the meaning or etymology of a name, treating the name as revelatory of the nature, character, or fate of its bearer.

This topic exploits the ancient belief that names were not arbitrary but meaningful - that they revealed something true about what was named.

## Examples

> "Another is from a name, for example, as in Sophocles, 'When [your mother] named you Sidero she clearly meant it.'" (1401a23-24)

Sidero means "iron" in Greek. The name reveals the cruel character: she was aptly named for her hard, iron nature.

Kennedy notes this is from a lost play about Tyro, "apparently addressing his cruel stepmother."

> "And [another example is] as people are accustomed to speak in praises of the gods." (1401a25)

Gods' names were conventionally analyzed for meaning. Kennedy suggests the missing example might be: "Zeus is given his name as the cause of life (zōēs)."

> "And as Conon called Thrasybulus thrasboulon ['bold in counsel']." (1401a25-26)

Thrasybulus's name breaks down into thrasy- (bold) and -boulos (counsel). His name marks him as "bold in counsel."

> "And as Herodicus said to Thrasymachus, 'You are always thrasymakhos ["bold in fight"]' and to Polus, 'You are always a polus ["a colt"],' and of Dracon the lawgiver that his laws were not those of a human being but of a drakon ['snake']; for they were harsh." (1401a26-30)

Multiple examples:
- Thrasymachus = "bold fighter" - his argumentative style fits his name
- Polus = "colt" - perhaps young and spirited
- Dracon = "snake/dragon" - his harsh laws fit a serpentine nature

> "And [another example is] as Hecuba in Euripides says of Aphrodite, 'And rightly the name of the goddess begins like aphrosynē.'" (1401a30-1401b1)

Aphrodite's name sounds like aphrosynē (folly). Hecuba suggests this reveals something about the goddess of love.

> "And as Chaeremon [said] 'Pentheus, named from his future unhappy fate.'" (1401b1-3)

Pentheus contains penthos (sorrow, grief). His tragic end was foretold in his name.

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- A name has a clear meaning or etymology
- That meaning can be connected to the argument
- The audience will recognize or accept the etymology

Applications include:
- **Character assessment**: Names reveal nature
- **Praise/blame**: Names indicate virtue or vice
- **Prophecy/fate**: Names foreshadow destiny
- **Divine interpretation**: Gods' names reveal their functions

The topic works best in cultures that take names seriously as meaningful, not arbitrary labels.

## Notes

This topic reflects ancient Greek name-culture, where names were often chosen for meaning and were thought to reveal truth about their bearers. The practice of deriving arguments from names appears throughout Greek literature, from Homer onward.

The puns and etymologies cited may be folk etymologies rather than historically accurate derivations, but rhetorical force depends on audience acceptance, not linguistic truth.
