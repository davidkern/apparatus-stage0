## Description

The twenty-third common topic addresses prejudged situations by explaining the cause of the false impression (to legein tēn aitian tou paradoxou - "stating the cause of the paradox/unexpected"). When someone or something has been misjudged, the speaker explains why the false belief arose, thereby neutralizing it.

The logic: "There is some reason why it seems true" - but that reason is not the actual situation. Once the cause of the mistaken appearance is explained, the false impression loses its force.

## Examples

> "Another, in reference to human beings or actions that have been prejudged or seem to have been, is to state the cause of the false impression; for there is some reason why it seems true." (1400b11-14)

Aristotle establishes the principle: false impressions have causes, and naming those causes undermines them.

> "For example, when a certain woman claimed that her son was the child of a different mother, because she embraced him she was thought to be involved with him as her lover, but when the reason was explained [i.e., that he really was her son] she was freed from slander." (1400b14-18)

The structure:
- False impression: Woman is sexually involved with young man
- Cause of impression: She was seen embracing him
- True explanation: He is her son (from a different mother than assumed)
- Once explained: The slander dissolves

The embrace seemed like evidence of an affair, but it was actually maternal affection.

> "Another example is in the Ajax of Theodectes: Odysseus tells Ajax why he [Odysseus] does not seem braver than Ajax, although he really is." (1400b18-21)

Odysseus explains why appearances deceive: He actually is braver, but something explains why it doesn't seem that way. (The specifics are not given, but the structure is clear: explain away the false impression.)

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- You or your client has been prejudged negatively
- The negative impression has a plausible cause
- Explaining that cause will dissolve the prejudice

The technique works because prejudice often stems from incomplete information or misinterpreted evidence. By supplying the missing context, the speaker transforms the apparent evidence into something innocent.

Key requirements:
1. Identify what caused the false impression
2. Explain why that cause led to the wrong conclusion
3. Present the true situation that the cause actually indicates

The topic is particularly useful in defensive rhetoric where reputation is at stake.

## Notes

The woman-and-son example is structurally rich: an embrace is ambiguous evidence. Without context, it seems to indicate intimacy; with context (he's her son), it indicates exactly what it should (maternal affection). The same action supports opposite conclusions depending on knowledge of the underlying relationship.

This topic connects to Aristotle's broader interest in how appearances (phainomena) relate to truth - a theme throughout his philosophical work.
