## Description

The third type of apparent enthymeme constructs or demolishes arguments through exaggeration (deinōsis). The speaker amplifies the seriousness (or triviality) of an action without actually proving whether the action occurred. The intensity of expression substitutes for demonstration.

This works in both directions:
- The accused can amplify so dramatically that it seems impossible they would do such a thing
- The accuser can rage so intensely that guilt seems obvious

In either case, no actual proof is offered - only emotional amplification.

## Examples

> "Another topic is constructing or demolishing an argument by exaggeration. This occurs when one amplifies the action without showing that it was performed." (1401b9-11)

Aristotle identifies the core problem: amplification without proof.

> "When [the accused] amplifies the charge, he causes it to appear that he has not committed the action." (1401b11-13)

Defensive use: "Could I have done something THIS terrible? Obviously not!" The very enormity of the amplified charge seems to make guilt implausible.

> "Or when the accuser goes into a rage [he makes it appear] that [the defendant] has." (1401b13-14)

Prosecutorial use: The accuser's emotional intensity suggests the defendant must be guilty to provoke such outrage.

> "There is then no enthymeme; for the hearer falsely reckons that he did it or did not, although this has not been shown." (1401b14-16)

Aristotle is explicit: this is NOT a valid enthymeme. The audience draws conclusions from emotional display rather than logical demonstration.

## Usage

**For recognition:**
- Notice when speakers dwell on the severity of charges without proving them
- Be alert to emotional intensity substituting for evidence
- Ask: "Was this SHOWN to have happened, or just amplified?"

**Both sides can use this:**
- Defense: Amplify the charge to make it seem too extreme to be true
- Prosecution: Amplify outrage to make guilt seem obvious

**The fallacy:**
- Audiences mistake emotional conviction for factual proof
- The "shape" of outrage or incredulity creates apparent conclusions
- No actual demonstration occurs

## Notes

This fallacy exploits the connection between emotion and belief. When people feel strongly, they tend to believe the feeling is justified by facts. Skilled speakers can generate feelings without providing facts.

This relates to but differs from legitimate amplification (auxesis) discussed in Chapter 26. Legitimate amplification enhances proved facts; fallacious amplification substitutes for proof.
