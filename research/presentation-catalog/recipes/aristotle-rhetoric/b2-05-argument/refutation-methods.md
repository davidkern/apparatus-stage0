## Description

Refutation (lysis - literally "unloosing" or "undoing") is the art of defeating an opponent's enthymeme. Aristotle identifies two primary methods:

1. **Counter-syllogism**: Constructing an opposite enthymeme using the same topics
2. **Objection (enstasis)**: Challenging a premise in the opponent's argument

The first method uses the opponent's own argumentative territory against them; the second directly attacks the foundations of their reasoning.

## Examples

> "The next point in the continuing discussion is to speak about refutation. It is possible to refute either by stating an opposite syllogism or by bringing an objection." (1402b12-14)

Aristotle introduces the two methods.

> "Now, clearly, an opposite syllogism can be made from the same topics [as the opponent used but drawing the opposite conclusion]; for the syllogisms are derived from commonly held opinions [endoxa] and many opinions are opposed to each other." (1402b14-17)

Why counter-syllogisms work: the same topics (common argumentative strategies) can generate opposing conclusions because commonly held opinions often conflict.

## Usage

**Counter-syllogism approach:**
- Identify the topic (argumentative strategy) the opponent is using
- Construct an enthymeme from the same topic that reaches the opposite conclusion
- This is possible because endoxa (accepted opinions) often support opposing views

**Objection approach:**
- Identify a premise in the opponent's enthymeme
- Challenge that premise directly
- See the four kinds of objection for specific methods

The choice between methods depends on:
- Whether the opponent's argument can be turned against itself
- Whether a vulnerable premise can be identified
- The rhetorical situation and available evidence

## Notes

The term lysis ("unloosing") captures the image of untying the logical connections that hold an enthymeme together. Whether by counter-argument or direct attack on premises, the goal is to dissolve the opponent's reasoning.
