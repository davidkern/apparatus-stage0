## Description

The seventh common topic argues from definition (horismos). The speaker defines a term, then draws syllogistic conclusions from that definition. This topic gives the speaker control over the conceptual framework - by defining terms strategically, conclusions follow.

Aristotle summarizes: "For all these [speakers], by making definitions and grasping the essence of a thing, draw syllogistic conclusions about the subject they are discussing."

## Examples

> "What is the divine? Is it not either a god or the work of a god? Still, whoever thinks it is the work of god must also think that gods exist." (1398a32-35)

An argument for the existence of gods from the definition of "divine." Kennedy notes this is "an apparent reference to Socrates' argument in Plato, Apology 27b-c."

> "[As] Iphicrates [argued], that the best person is the most noble; for there was no noble quality in Harmodius and Aristogiton until they did something noble, while he himself was more like them [than his opponent was]: 'At least, my deeds are more like those of Harmodius and Aristogiton than yours are.'" (1398a35-1398b1)

Iphicrates redefines "noble" (eugenes) away from birth toward action. By this definition, nobility comes from noble deeds, not ancestry - and his deeds prove him nobler than his aristocratic opponent.

> "[As said] in the Alexander, that all would agree that those who are not well-behaved are not content with the enjoyment of one [woman's] body." (1398b1-3)

A definition of "well-behaved" that Paris allegedly fails to meet.

> "[Another is] the reason Socrates gave for refusing to visit Archelaus; for he said hybris was just as much an inability on the part of those benefited to return a favor as [it was retaliation by] those harmed." (1398b3-5)

Socrates redefines hybris (outrage, insolence) to include being unable to reciprocate favors. By this definition, accepting lavish gifts from King Archelaus would be hybris - hence the refusal.

## Usage

The power of this topic lies in controlling definitions. Strategies include:

1. **Essential definition**: Define by the nature of the thing
2. **Reframing definition**: Redefine a term to include or exclude relevant cases
3. **Etymological definition**: Define by the word's origin (see Topic 28)

Once a definition is established (or assumed), syllogistic conclusions follow automatically. The Iphicrates example shows how redefining a contested term can reverse the apparent advantage of an opponent.

Use this topic when:
- A key term's definition is in dispute or can be strategically shaped
- Conclusions can be drawn directly from definition
- The audience will accept the proposed definition

## Notes

This topic connects rhetoric to dialectic and philosophy. Definition is central to Socratic/Platonic method, and Aristotle treats definition extensively in the Topics and Metaphysics. The rhetorical application focuses on persuasive definitions that advance the speaker's case.
