## Description

The first type of apparent (fallacious) enthymeme arises from verbal style (para tēn lexin). When a statement is expressed compactly and antithetically - in the balanced, contrasting style characteristic of valid enthymemes - it APPEARS to be an enthymeme even when the logical connection is missing.

Aristotle's point: the rhetorical form (lexis) of an enthymeme can exist without the logical substance. Audiences mistake the shape for the thing.

## Examples

> "One kind of topoi of apparent enthymemes is verbal and of this one part resembles what occurs in dialectic when the final statement takes the form of a conclusion without constituting a [valid] syllogism ('since such and such [is true], necessarily also this and that follows')." (1401a1-5)

The verbal markers of syllogism ("since... necessarily...") appear without the logical validity.

> "In the case of enthymemes a statement appears to be an enthymeme whenever it is spoken compactly and antithetically; for verbal style of this sort is the place where an enthymeme is at home, and such seems to result from the shape of the expression." (1401a5-9)

The key insight: enthymemes have a characteristic STYLE (compact, antithetical). That style can be imitated without the logic.

> "For purposes of speaking in verbally [i.e., not logically valid] syllogistic form it is useful to enumerate the headings of several syllogisms; [for example,] that [Evagoras] saved some, avenged others, and freed the Greeks. Each of these statements has been demonstrated from other arguments, but when they are put together something else appears to result from them." (1401a9-14)

The example from Isocrates' Evagoras:
- Evagoras saved some (proven separately)
- Evagoras avenged others (proven separately)
- Evagoras freed the Greeks (proven separately)

When combined compactly, a new conclusion seems to emerge (that Evagoras was brave/virtuous/great). But the mere accumulation of headings doesn't validly prove the generalization.

## Usage

**For recognition (primary use):**
- Be alert when arguments rely heavily on stylistic parallelism
- Check whether the logical connection exists, not just the verbal form
- Accumulated premises don't automatically generate new conclusions

**The danger:**
- Audiences are conditioned to associate compact, antithetical style with valid reasoning
- Speakers can exploit this by using the style without the substance
- The "shape of expression" substitutes for actual inference

Kennedy notes this draws on Isocrates' Evagoras (65-69), showing how even respected orators employed this technique.

## Notes

This fallacy reveals Aristotle's awareness that rhetoric operates through both logos (logical content) and lexis (verbal style). The two can come apart: valid style without valid logic, or valid logic in poor style.

The reference to dialectic shows the parallel in philosophical argument: the verbal form "since X, necessarily Y" can be spoken without X actually entailing Y.
