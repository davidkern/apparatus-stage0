## Description

The twentieth common topic examines what turns the mind toward or away from action (ta protreptonta kai apotreptonta - "incentives and deterrents"). This topic analyzes the factors that would encourage or discourage someone from acting, then argues about likely behavior or actual guilt based on this analysis.

Aristotle notes this topic is "common both to litigants and deliberative speakers" and constitutes "the whole Art of Pamphilus and Callippus."

## Examples

> "Another that is common both to litigants and deliberative speakers is to look at what turns the mind in favor and what turns the mind against something and for what reasons people both act and avoid action." (1400a15-18)

Aristotle establishes the scope: this applies to both judicial and deliberative rhetoric.

> "For these are the factors that if present, impel action [but if not present, deter action]; for example, [consider] if [an action was] possible and easy and advantageous to a person or friends or harmful to enemies and, if punishable, [consider whether] the punishment is less than the reward of the action." (1400a18-22)

The factors to analyze:
- **Possibility**: Could they have done it?
- **Ease**: Was it easy to do?
- **Advantage**: Did it benefit them or their friends?
- **Harm to enemies**: Did it hurt their opponents?
- **Risk-reward**: Was potential punishment less than potential gain?

> "People are urged on for these reasons and dissuaded by their opposites, and they use these same arguments in accusation and defense: they defend themselves by drawing on reasons that deter, and they prosecute by drawing on those that encourage." (1400a22-25)

The bidirectional application:
- **Prosecution**: Emphasize incentives - they had every reason to do it
- **Defense**: Emphasize deterrents - they had every reason not to do it

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- Evaluating whether someone likely committed an action (judicial)
- Advising whether someone should undertake an action (deliberative)
- Assessing character based on choices (epideictic)

The analysis considers:
1. **Possibility and ease**: Opportunity
2. **Benefit to self/friends**: Motive
3. **Harm to enemies**: Motive
4. **Risk vs. reward**: Rational calculation

For prosecution: Show the action was possible, easy, beneficial, and low-risk.
For defense: Show the action was difficult, risky, or contrary to the defendant's interests.

## Notes

Kennedy identifies Pamphilus as "a little-known earlier rhetorician, whose handbook is described by Cicero (On the Orator 3.82), probably derived from Aristotle's Synagoge, as 'puerile.'"

That two rhetorical handbooks were built entirely around this topic suggests its fundamental importance to forensic and deliberative practice. The topic provides a systematic framework for analyzing human motivation.
