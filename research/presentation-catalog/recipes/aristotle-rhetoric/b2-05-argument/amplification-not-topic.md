## Description

Aristotle clarifies a taxonomic point: amplification (auxesis) and deprecation (meioun) are NOT elements or topics of enthymemes. Rather, they are ways of USING the topics to make something appear greater or smaller.

This matters for understanding the structure of rhetorical invention: topics generate arguments, while amplification/deprecation are techniques for applying those arguments to questions of magnitude.

Similarly, refutative enthymemes are not a separate species (they use the same topics as demonstrative enthymemes, just for opposite purposes), and objections (enstaseis) are not enthymemes at all but challenges to premises.

## Examples

> "To amplify or to deprecate is not an element of an enthymeme. (I call the same things 'element' and 'topic'; for an element or a topic [is a heading] under which many enthymemes fall.)" (1403a16-19)

Aristotle explains his terminology: element = topic = a general strategy that generates multiple specific arguments.

> "To amplify and to deprecate contribute to showing that something is great or small, just as also [to showing that something is] good or evil or just or unjust and anything else, but all these things are the subjects of syllogisms and enthymemes, so if each of them is not a topic of an enthymeme, neither is amplification and deprecation." (1403a19-24)

The argument: Just as "good/evil" and "just/unjust" are SUBJECTS of arguments (not topics for generating arguments), so "great/small" is a subject. Amplification helps prove something is great; it doesn't provide the logical structure for that proof.

> "Nor are refutative enthymemes a distinct species; for clearly, one refutes by proving something or bringing an objection, but these are a demonstration in response to the adversary's position." (1403a24-28)

Refutation is not special: it's just demonstration (or objection) pointed at the opponent's claims.

> "'Objection' is not an enthymeme either; but, as in the Topics, it is a matter of stating an opinion from which it will be clear that the opponent's argument does not constitute a syllogism or that he has introduced something false." (1403a28-32)

Objections are not themselves arguments but challenges to arguments - they reveal flaws rather than construct proofs.

## Usage

This recipe clarifies what IS and IS NOT a topic:

**Topics (elements) ARE:**
- General strategies for finding arguments (the 28 topics of Chapter 23)
- Headings under which many enthymemes fall
- Sources of enthymematic structure

**Topics ARE NOT:**
- Amplification/deprecation (uses of topics, not sources)
- Refutation (application of topics to opponent's arguments)
- Objection (challenge to premises, not argument construction)

**Practical implication:**
When you amplify, you're using one or more of the 28 topics to make something SEEM greater. The amplification is in how you use the topic, not a separate argumentative strategy.

## Notes

This chapter serves a clarificatory function, preventing category confusion. It also transitions from Book 2 (invention/thought) to Book 3 (style and arrangement):

> "Since there are in fact three things that should be systematically worked out in discussion of speech, let us regard what has been said as enough about paradigms and maxims and enthymemes and in general about the thought and the sources of argument and how we shall refute them. It remains to go through an account of style and arrangement."

The chapter marks the completion of Aristotle's treatment of invention (heuresis/dianoia) before turning to lexis (style) and taxis (arrangement).
