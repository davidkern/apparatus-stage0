## Description

The twenty-first common topic argues from the implausible (ek tōn apistōn). Paradoxically, the very implausibility of a claim can serve as evidence for its truth. The reasoning: people accept facts or probabilities as true; if something were implausible and not probable, it must be true - because only truth would explain why anyone would assert something so unlikely.

This is sometimes called the "argument from incredibility" - something so strange that no one would make it up.

## Examples

> "Another is derived from things that are thought to have taken place but yet are implausible, [using the argument] that they would not seem true unless they were facts or close to being facts." (1400a23-25)

Aristotle presents the core logic: implausible things wouldn't be believed unless they were (or were close to being) true.

> "And [one can argue] that they are all the more true [for that reason]; for people accept facts or probabilities as true; if, then, something were implausible and not probable, it would be true; for it is not because of probability and plausibility that it seems true [but because it is a fact]." (1400a25-28)

The extended reasoning: We normally believe things because they're probable. If we believe something improbable, it must be because it's factually true, not because it's likely.

> "For example, when Andocles of Pittheus was speaking against a law and said, 'Laws need a law to correct them,' there was general outcry against him." (1400a28-30)

The implausible claim: laws need laws to correct them (this seems paradoxical - infinite regress?).

> "[He continued,] 'And fishes need salt' (although it is neither probable nor credible that creatures born in the sea would need salt) 'and pressed olives need oil' (though it is strange that things from which oil comes would need oil)." (1400a30-1400b3)

Andocles provides parallels that are implausible yet true:
- Fish (which live in saltwater) need salt (for preservation)
- Olives (which produce oil) need oil (in processing)

The implausibility doesn't prevent the truth. Similarly, laws may need meta-laws to correct them, despite the initial paradox.

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- You need to support a claim that seems unlikely
- The claim's implausibility can be reframed as evidence
- You can show there's no motive to fabricate something so unlikely

The argument works by shifting the burden: "Why would anyone claim this unless it were true?"

The technique is particularly effective when:
- Simpler lies would be more believable
- The implausible claim is self-damaging (no benefit to lying)
- Parallel examples of true-but-implausible things exist

## Notes

Kennedy compares this to Tertullian's famous (though likely misattributed) claim of believing "because it is absurd." The rhetorical force comes from inverting normal expectations: implausibility becomes evidence rather than counter-evidence.

The Andocles example is particularly clever: he uses analogies (fish and salt, olives and oil) that share the same paradoxical structure as his main claim, validating the pattern.
