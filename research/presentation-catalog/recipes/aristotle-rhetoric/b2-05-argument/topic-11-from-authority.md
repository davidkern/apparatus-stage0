## Description

The eleventh common topic argues from a previous judgment (krisis). The speaker cites what respected authorities have decided about the same or similar matters, treating their judgment as support for the current conclusion.

Aristotle provides a hierarchy of authorities, from strongest to weakest:
1. All people always
2. Most people
3. The wise (all or most)
4. The good
5. The judges themselves
6. Those whom the judges approve
7. Those with legal authority
8. Those it would be shameful to contradict (gods, fathers, teachers)

## Examples

> "Another [topic] is from a [previous] judgment about the same or a similar or opposite matter, especially if all always [make this judgment] - but if not, at least most people, or the wise (either all of them or most) or the good; or if the judges themselves [have so decided] or those whom the judges approve or those whose judgment cannot be opposed, for example, those with legal authority to make it or whose judgment cannot be honorably opposed, for example, the gods, a father, or teachers." (1398b30-1399a1)

Aristotle sets out the hierarchy of authoritative judgments.

> "[As] Autocles said against Mixidemides: 'If it is right for the Dread Goddesses to stand trial in the Areopagus, should not Mixidemides?'" (1399a1-3)

The Dread Goddesses (the Furies) were prosecutors in mythological/dramatic tradition. If divine beings can stand trial, certainly a mortal should.

> "Or as Sappho said, that it is bad to die; for the gods have so judged; for otherwise they would die." (1399a3-4)

The gods' immortality is evidence of their judgment that death is bad.

> "Or as Aristippus [replied] to Plato, when the latter said something rather dogmatic, as he thought: 'But our companion,' he said, 'would have said nothing of the sort,' meaning Socrates." (1399a4-7)

Invoking Socrates as an authority against Plato - using a shared respected figure.

> "And at Delphi Agesipolis, after earlier consulting oracles at Olympia, asked the god if his opinion was the same as his father's, implying it would be shameful for him to say contradictory things." (1399a7-9)

A clever use of authority: putting Apollo in a position where disagreeing with Zeus would be shameful.

> "And as Isocrates wrote about Helen, that she was virtuous, since Theseus so judged; and about Alexander, whom the goddesses preferred to others; and about Evagoras, that he was virtuous, so Isocrates claims: for at any rate Conon, leaving all others aside, came to him [for help]." (1399a9-13)

Multiple applications from Isocrates: Theseus's choice validates Helen; the goddesses' choice validates Paris; Conon's choice validates Evagoras.

## Usage

Use this topic when:
- A respected authority has made a relevant judgment
- The current case is parallel to what the authority judged
- The audience accepts the authority's credibility

The hierarchy suggests strategy:
- **Strongest**: Universal agreement ("all always")
- **Strong**: Majority opinion, wise consensus
- **Moderate**: Specific respected individuals
- **Situational**: Judges' own previous decisions, legal precedent

The technique can also be used reflexively - citing the judges' own past judgments creates pressure for consistency.

## Notes

Kennedy notes the phraseology here "is reminiscent of the basis of dialectic in generally accepted opinions as described in Topics 1.1." The connection between rhetorical authority and dialectical endoxa (accepted opinions) is significant - both ground argument in what is already believed.

The Agesipolis example shows sophisticated manipulation of authority - structuring a question so that the authority cannot give an unfavorable answer without self-contradiction.
