## Description

The first of Aristotle's twenty-eight common topics (topoi koinoi) for constructing enthymemes. This topic instructs the speaker to examine whether the opposite predicate is true of the opposite subject. If so, the argument is confirmed; if not, it is refuted.

The logical structure is: if the opposite of P is true of the opposite of S, then P is true of S. The power lies in the implicit premise that opposites naturally yield opposite results, which audiences accept without explicit statement.

This topic works bidirectionally: it constructs arguments by establishing symmetry, and it refutes arguments by showing the expected symmetry fails.

## Examples

> "One should look to see if the opposite [predicate] is true of the opposite [subject], [thus] refuting the argument if it is not, confirming it if it is: for example, that to be temperate is a good thing; for to lack self control is harmful." (1397a8-10)

The argument: lack of self-control (opposite of temperance) is harmful (opposite of good). Therefore, temperance is good.

> "If the war is the cause of present evils, things should be set right by making peace." (1397a12-13)

From the Messeniacus of Alcidamas. War causes evils; therefore its opposite (peace) should cause the opposite (setting things right).

> "For since it is unjust to fall into anger / At those who have unwillingly done wrong, / If someone benefits another perforce / It is not appropriate for thanks to be owed." (1397a14-17)

A poetic example showing the structure: if it's unjust to be angry at unwilling wrongdoers, it's not appropriate to thank unwilling benefactors.

> "But since, old man, false statements are persuasive / Among mortals, you should believe the opposite too: / That many truths turn out to be incredible to mortals." (1397a19-21)

From Euripides' Thyestes. If false statements can seem persuasive, then by the same logic truths can seem incredible.

## Usage

Most effective when:
- The opposites are obvious and uncontroversial
- The consequences are well-established or easily accepted
- The audience is predisposed to accept symmetry

Can be used in two modes:
1. **Confirming**: Show the opposite predicate holds for the opposite subject, therefore the original claim holds
2. **Refuting**: Show the opposite predicate does NOT hold for the opposite subject, therefore the original claim fails

Aristotle lists this first among the 28 topics, indicating its fundamental importance to enthymematic reasoning.

## Notes

This topic appears frequently in Aristotle's dialectical treatise Topics (113b15ff) where it receives fuller treatment. The rhetorical application emphasizes the implicit nature of the reasoning - the audience supplies the premise that opposites yield opposite results.
