## Description

Enthymemes are drawn from four sources, and each type has different vulnerabilities to refutation:

1. **Probability (eikos)**: What is usually but not always true - refutable by showing exceptions
2. **Example/Paradigm**: Induction from particular instances - refutable by counter-examples
3. **Sign (semeion)**: Fallible indicators - refutable as non-syllogistic
4. **Necessary sign (tekmerion)**: Infallible indicators - only refutable by denying the fact

Aristotle notes that defendants have an inherent advantage because most arguments rely on probability, which can always be objected to.

## Examples

> "Since enthymemes are drawn from four sources and these four are probability, paradigm, tekmerion, and semeion... it is clear that all these kinds of enthymemes can be refuted by bringing objections, but the refutation may be an apparent one, not always valid." (1402b32-1403a1)

Aristotle establishes the four sources.

### Refuting probability arguments:

> "For the objector does not refute the probability but [shows] that [the conclusion in a particular case] is not necessary." (1403a1-3)

The key insight: objecting to probability doesn't prove the opposite - it only shows the conclusion isn't certain.

> "As a further result, the defendant always has an advantage over the prosecutor because of this fallacy; for since the prosecutor demonstrates by probabilities, and since it is not the same thing to show in refutation that an argument is not probable as to show it is not necessary, what is for the most part true is always open to objection; for otherwise it would not be for the most part and probable, but always and necessary." (1403a3-10)

The defendant's advantage:
- Prosecution relies on probabilities (X probably did it)
- Defense shows the conclusion isn't NECESSARY (X might not have done it)
- This creates reasonable doubt, even if the probability remains high

> "Yet when a refutation is made in this way, the judge thinks either that the thing is not probable or that it is not for him to decide, reasoning falsely... Therefore, it is not sufficient if one attacks an argument as 'not necessary'; and one must refute it as 'not probable.'" (1403a10-15)

A warning for prosecutors: Jurors may mistake "not necessary" for "not probable." To truly refute, you must show the claim isn't even probable - that your objection is MORE probable than the original claim.

### Refuting example-based arguments:

> "The refutation of examples is the same as that of probabilities; for if we cite an example that does not accord with the [generalized] conclusion, the argument is refuted because it is not 'necessary,' even if something else is more often true or true in more cases." (1403a15-19)

One counter-example defeats the claim of necessity. But if the majority of cases support the opponent, argue that "the present case is not similar or not in the same way or has some difference."

### Refuting sign-based arguments:

> "Signs and enthymemes that draw a conclusion through a sign are refutable, even if true, as was said in the first [lectures]; for that every refutable sign is non-syllogistic is clear to us from the Analytics." (1403a19-23)

Signs are inherently non-syllogistic (they don't prove conclusions necessarily). Even if the sign is true, the inference can be challenged.

### Refuting necessary sign (tekmerion) arguments:

> "But tekmeria and enthymemes with infallible signs cannot be refuted as non-syllogistic... and what is left is to show that the alleged fact is not true. If it is evident that it is true and that there is an infallible sign of it, the argument is irrefutable from the start; for the whole demonstration is already evident." (1403a23-30)

Necessary signs cannot be challenged logically - they prove their conclusions. The only recourse is to deny that the sign actually exists (deny the fact).

## Usage

**Against probability arguments:**
- Show exceptions exist (the conclusion isn't necessary)
- Better: Show your objection is MORE probable
- Remember: Judges may conflate "not necessary" with "not probable"

**Against example-based arguments:**
- Provide counter-examples
- Argue the present case differs from the cited examples

**Against sign-based arguments:**
- Challenge the inference, not just the sign
- Show the sign could indicate something else

**Against necessary signs:**
- Only option: Deny the fact exists
- If the sign is confirmed, the argument is irrefutable

## Notes

The "defendant's advantage" Aristotle identifies has significant implications for judicial rhetoric: the structure of proof by probability inherently favors the defense, who need only create doubt. This connects to later development of concepts like "reasonable doubt" in legal theory.

The reference to "the Analytics" shows Aristotle drawing on his logical work (Prior Analytics 2.27) for the technical analysis of signs and necessity.
