## Description

The eighth common topic argues from the varied meanings (posakhōs - "in how many ways") of a word. This topic recognizes that words often have multiple distinct senses, and the argument turns on distinguishing or exploiting these meanings.

Aristotle's treatment here is brief, referring to fuller discussion in his Topics. The topic can be used legitimately (to clarify which meaning applies) or sophisticaly (to confuse meanings) - the latter is addressed in Chapter 24 as a fallacy.

## Examples

> "Another is from the varied meanings [of a word], as discussed in the Topics on the meaning of oxus." (1398b5-7)

Kennedy notes: "Oxus (sharp) - in music the opposite of flat, of a knife the opposite of dull. See Topics 1.15."

The word "sharp" (oxus) has different meanings:
- Musical: high pitch (opposite of flat)
- Physical: keen edge (opposite of dull)
- Intellectual: quick-witted (opposite of slow)

An argument about whether something is "sharp" depends on which sense is in play.

## Usage

This topic operates in two modes:

**Disambiguation (legitimate):**
- Clarify which meaning of an ambiguous term applies
- Show that an opponent's argument trades on the wrong meaning
- Establish the relevant sense for the case at hand

**Exploitation (potentially sophistical):**
- Shift between meanings to create apparent conclusions
- Use one meaning to establish a premise, another to draw the conclusion

The legitimate use is valuable for:
- Exposing equivocation in opponent's arguments
- Precision in defining terms of debate
- Showing that an apparent contradiction is merely verbal

## Notes

This is the briefest of the 28 topics, with Aristotle referring readers to his Topics (1.15) for fuller treatment. The brevity likely reflects that the topic is well-covered in his dialectical work.

The relationship between this topic and the fallacy from homonyms (Chapter 24) is important: the same underlying phenomenon (word with multiple meanings) can be used validly or fallaciously depending on whether the speaker clearly distinguishes the senses.
