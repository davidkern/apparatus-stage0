## Description

The ninth type of apparent enthymeme confuses what is generally probable with what is probable in particular circumstances (para to haplōs kai mē haplōs - "simple/absolute vs. qualified"). What is improbable generally may be probable in specific cases, and vice versa. Sophistical argument exploits this confusion.

This fallacy is the basis of "making the weaker seem the better cause" - the notorious claim attributed to sophists like Protagoras.

## Examples

> "Just as in eristics an apparent syllogism occurs in confusing what is general and what is not general but some particular... so also in rhetoric there is an apparent enthymeme in regard to what is not generally probable but probable in a particular case." (1402a19-23)

The principle: particular probabilities can be mistaken for general ones.

> "Probably one could say that this thing itself is probable: / Many improbable things happen to mortals." (1402a23-25)

A quote from Agathon capturing the paradox: improbable things are probable (because improbable things happen all the time). The improbable becomes probable BECAUSE it's improbable.

> "For some things happen contrary to probability, so what is contrary to probability is also probable. If this is so, the improbable will be probable. But not generally so; as in eristics, not adding the circumstances and reference and manner makes for deception." (1402a25-30)

Aristotle exposes the trick: the improbable is probable in SPECIFIC circumstances, not generally. Omitting the qualifying circumstances creates the sophistry.

> "The Art of Corax is made up of this topic: for example, if a weak man were charged with assault, he should be acquitted as not being a likely suspect for the charge; for it is not probable [that a weak man would attack another]. And if he is a likely suspect, for example, if he is strong, [he should be acquitted]; for it is not likely [that he would start a fight] for the very reason that it was going to seem probable." (1402a30-1402b3)

Corax's double argument:
- If WEAK: "I'm too weak to have done it - improbable"
- If STRONG: "I knew I'd be suspected because I'm strong, so I wouldn't do it - improbable"

Either way, the defendant argues improbability. But only one of these is genuinely probable; the other exploits the confusion between particular and general.

> "This is 'to make the weaker seem the better cause.' Thus, people were rightly angry at the declaration of Protagoras; for it is a lie and not true but a fallacious probability and a part of no art except rhetoric and eristic." (1402b3-8)

Aristotle condemns the technique: it's a lie, it's fallacious, and it belongs only to sophistical rhetoric.

## Usage

**For recognition:**
- Notice when arguments claim improbability from EITHER direction
- Check whether the probability is general or particular to circumstances
- Ask: "Is this probably true generally, or only in these specific conditions?"

**The Corax double-argument:**
- Any characteristic can be used to argue improbability
- "I'm X, so it's improbable I did it"
- "I'm not-X, so it's improbable I did it"
- Both can't be simultaneously valid; at least one exploits particular vs. general confusion

## Notes

Kennedy notes: "Plato, Phaedrus 273a-b, attributes the following argument to Tisias; Corax ('Crow') is probably a nickname for Tisias of Syracuse."

Aristotle's strong language ("people were rightly angry") shows his disapproval of this sophistical technique. While he documents it, he condemns it as belonging only to eristic (combative) and deceptive rhetoric, not to genuine rhetorical art.
