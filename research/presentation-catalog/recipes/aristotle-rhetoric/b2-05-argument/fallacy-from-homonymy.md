## Description

Another verbal fallacy (part of the first type) exploits homonyms - words that sound the same but have different meanings. The speaker uses a term in one sense in the premise and another sense in the conclusion, creating an apparent connection that is merely verbal.

This is the fallacy of equivocation: the argument seems valid because the same word appears in multiple places, but its meaning shifts.

## Examples

> "Another [verbal fallacy] is by use of homonyms, as saying that a mouse [mys] is a worthy creature from which comes the most honored of all festivals; for the [celebration of the Eleusinian] Mysteries is the most honored of all." (1401a14-17)

The pun:
- mys = mouse
- mystēria = Mysteries (sacred rites)

These words sound similar but have completely different origins and meanings. The argument pretends a connection: mice are honored because Mysteries (sounds like "mouse-things") are honored.

> "Or if someone delivered an encomium of the dog that included the Dog Star or [the god] Pan, because Pindar said [of Pan], 'O blessed one, whom the Olympians call dog of the Great Goddess, taking every form.'" (1401a17-21)

"Dog" (kyōn) appears in:
- The animal
- The Dog Star (Sirius)
- An epithet for Pan

Praising dogs by praising the Dog Star or divine Pan trades on homonymy - the praise doesn't transfer to actual dogs.

> "Or [if someone said that] because it is most dishonorable for there to be no 'dog,' thus clearly the dog is honored." (1401a21-22)

Another play on "dog" - perhaps meaning "attendant" or some other honorable sense.

> "Or to say that Hermes is the 'most sociable' [koinōnikos] of the gods; for he alone is called 'Hermes the Sharer' [koinos]." (1401a22-24)

Hermes is called "koinos" (shared, common) because of a traditional exclamation when finding something valuable. This doesn't make him "sociable" (koinōnikos) in the intended sense.

> "And that logos is the best thing, because good men are worthy not of money but of logos ['esteem']; for 'worthy of logos' has more than one meaning." (1401a24-26)

Logos means:
- Speech/reason/argument
- Esteem/account/regard

"Good men deserve logos" shifts from "esteem" to "speech/reason," making speech seem supremely valuable by equivocation.

## Usage

**For recognition:**
- Identify when the same word appears in multiple parts of an argument
- Check whether the meaning is consistent across uses
- Be especially alert to puns, sound-alikes, and polysemous terms

**Common forms:**
- Sound-alikes treated as conceptually related (mys/mystēria)
- Metaphorical uses treated as literal (Hermes the "Sharer")
- Different senses of polysemous words conflated (logos as reason/esteem)

## Notes

Kennedy notes: "Mystery is not derived from mys (mouse), though they sound the same, but from the verb myein, 'to close the lips, keep secret.'" The folk etymology is false, but rhetorically exploitable.

This fallacy relates to the legitimate Topic 8 (from varied meanings) - the difference is whether the multiple meanings are explicitly distinguished (valid) or covertly conflated (fallacious).
