# Communicative Intents from Tufte Chapters 8-9 (pp. 161-179)

Source: Edward Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, Chapter 8 "Data Density and Small Multiples" and Chapter 9 "Aesthetics and Technique in Data Graphical Design" (opening section)

---

## Extracted Intents

### 1. Maximize Data Density

**Definition**: Pack the maximum amount of quantitative information into the smallest possible visual space, treating graphic area as a scarce resource to be used efficiently.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Maximize data density and the size of the data matrix, within reason" (p. 168). Data density is measured as "numbers per square inch" and Tufte provides extensive comparative data on publication practices.

**Examples from text**:
- Annual sunshine record: ~1,000 numbers per square inch (p. 165)
- Map of French communes: ~9,000 numbers per square inch, encoding 240,000 numbers total (p. 166)
- Galaxy map: 110,000 numbers per square inch — "the current record" (p. 166)
- Comparison table showing Nature (48 median) vs. Pravda (0.2 median) data densities (p. 167)

**Framework category**: **Aggregative** — High density is a form of aggregation that makes vast datasets comprehensible.

---

### 2. Enable Within-View Comparison

**Definition**: Display enough information within a single visual field that the eye can compare parts of the data without requiring memory or page-turning.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "High-density graphics help us to compare parts of the data by displaying much information within the view of the eye: we look at one page at a time and the more on the page, the more effective and comparative our eye can be" (p. 168)

**Examples from text**:
- Reference to x-ray analysis: "search a reduced image so that the whole display can be perceived on at least one occasion without large eye movement" (p. 168)
- Small multiples enabling comparison across all frames simultaneously

**Framework category**: **Comparative** — The intent is to enable comparison; density is the mechanism.

---

### 3. Establish Credibility Through Data Richness

**Definition**: Use comprehensive data display to establish trust and transparency, as sparse displays raise suspicions about what has been omitted.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Data-rich designs give a context and credibility to statistical evidence. Low-information designs are suspect: what is left out, what is hidden, why are we shown so little?" (p. 168)

**Examples from text**:
- The implicit contrast between high-density scientific graphics (Nature, Journal of Royal Statistical Society) and low-density publications
- The argument that "more information is better than less information" (p. 168)

**Framework category**: **Evidential** — Data richness serves evidential integrity by demonstrating nothing is hidden.

---

### 4. Shrink Graphics

**Definition**: Reduce the physical size of graphics to increase data density and enable more information on a single page.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Graphics can be shrunk way down" — the Shrink Principle. "Many data graphics can be reduced in area to half their currently published size with virtually no loss in legibility and information" (p. 169)

**Examples from text**:
- Bertin's page with 17 small-scale graphics plus extensive text (p. 169)
- The progression from shrinking to small multiples as a design strategy

**Framework category**: **Aggregative** — Shrinking is a means to the aggregative end of showing more data.

---

### 5. Show Change Over an Index Variable (Small Multiples)

**Definition**: Display a series of graphics showing the same variables indexed by changes in another variable, enabling perception of how relationships shift.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Small multiples resemble the frames of a movie: a series of graphics, showing the same combination of variables, indexed by changes in another variable" (p. 170)

**Examples from text**:
- Los Angeles air pollution: 23 hourly frames showing hydrocarbon emission distribution (p. 170)
- Melanoma distribution on head/neck, split by gender (p. 171)
- Herring catch age composition 1908-1913, showing 1904 cohort aging through catches (p. 172)
- Chromosome comparison across man, chimpanzee, gorilla, orangutan (p. 172-173)
- Consumer Reports repair records across years and trouble spots (p. 174)

**Framework category**: **Temporal** (when index is time) or **Comparative** (when index is category) — Small multiples serve both purposes depending on the indexing variable.

---

### 6. Maintain Design Constancy Across Frames

**Definition**: Keep the graphical design constant across all panels of a small multiple so that attention is devoted entirely to data shifts rather than design interpretation.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "The design remains constant through all the frames, so that attention is devoted entirely to shifts in the data" (p. 170)

**Examples from text**:
- LA air pollution maps: same geographic outline, color scale, and layout across all 23 hours
- Herring catches: same axis scales and visual format across all 6 years
- Consumer Reports: identical row/column structure across all vehicle types

**Framework category**: **Comparative** — Constancy enables comparison by eliminating design as a variable.

---

### 7. Reveal Interaction Effects

**Definition**: Show how the relationship between variables changes as a third (or fourth) variable changes, revealing multiplicative or interaction effects.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Small multiples are "often narrative in content, showing shifts in the relationship between variables as the index variable changes (thereby revealing interaction or multiplicative effects)" (p. 175)

**Examples from text**:
- Herring age composition showing how the dominant 1904 cohort appears at different ages in successive years
- Melanoma distribution showing gender-specific patterns (women: more on face; men: more on scalp/ears)

**Framework category**: **Relational** — Interaction effects are a form of complex relationship among multiple variables.

---

### 8. Perform Data Graphical Arithmetic

**Definition**: Use graphic composition to show addition, subtraction, or decomposition of data — the whole equals the sum of its parts.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Notes "data graphical arithmetic, similar to that of the multiwindow plot" (p. 171) in reference to melanoma distribution.

**Examples from text**:
- Melanoma map: total distribution = male distribution + female distribution (p. 171)
- The three-panel structure showing combined, then gender-separated views

**Framework category**: **Comparative** / **Aggregative** — Shows how parts compose into wholes.

---

### 9. Create Narrative Through Sequence

**Definition**: Use ordered sequence of graphics to tell a story about change, causation, or development.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Small multiples are "often narrative in content" (p. 175)

**Examples from text**:
- Herring catches telling the story of the 1904 cohort as it ages from 4-year-olds to 9-year-olds
- LA air pollution showing the daily buildup and dissipation of emissions
- The implicit narrative of Consumer Reports showing reliability trends over time

**Framework category**: **Temporal** — Narrative is inherently temporal, unfolding through sequence.

---

### 10. Match Visual Metaphor to Data Meaning

**Definition**: Align the visual treatment with the semantic meaning of what is being shown, so that visual perception naturally corresponds to data interpretation.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "The visual metaphor corresponds appropriately to the data if the image is reversed, so that the light areas are the times when the sun shines" (p. 165)

**Examples from text**:
- Sunshine record: reversing the image so light = sunshine, dark = no sunshine
- The transparent flow-lines in Minard's Hannibal map allowing underlying geography to show through (p. 177)

**Framework category**: New category suggestion: **Metaphorical Correspondence** or subsumed under **Evidential** — the visual encoding should not contradict semantic meaning.

---

### 11. Achieve Elegance Through Simplicity of Design and Complexity of Data

**Definition**: Create visual excellence by combining minimal design apparatus with maximal data content.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Graphical elegance is often found in simplicity of design and complexity of data" (p. 177)

**Examples from text**:
- Minard's graphics praised for quality alongside Playfair and Marey
- Galaxy map cited as excellent recent work
- The aphorisms: "For non-data-ink, less is more. For data-ink, less is a bore" (p. 175)

**Framework category**: This is a meta-principle about design rather than a specific communicative intent.

---

### 12. Engage Significant Subject Matter

**Definition**: Apply graphical excellence to topics that matter — life, death, the universe — rather than trivial subjects.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "The best graphics are about the useful and important, about life and death, about the universe. Beautiful graphics do not traffic with the trivial" (p. 177)

**Examples from text**:
- Minard's graphics of army deaths in Russian campaign
- Cancer mortality maps
- Galaxy distribution mapping cosmic structure

**Framework category**: This is a meta-principle about topic selection rather than a specific communicative intent.

---

### 13. Facilitate Comparison Through Format Choice

**Definition**: Select the presentation format (sentence, text-table, table, semi-graphic, or graphic) that best supports the comparisons the data requires.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "The conventional sentence is a poor way to show more than two numbers because it prevents comparisons within the data" (p. 178). The recommendation to use text-tables and ordered layouts.

**Examples from text**:
- Transformation from "Nearly 53 percent of the type A group..." (hard to compare) to vertically aligned Group B 46% / Group A 53% / Group C 57% (easy to compare) (p. 178)
- The "supertable" for How Different Groups Voted: "One supertable is far better than a hundred little bar charts" (p. 179)

**Framework category**: **Comparative** — The format choice serves comparative purpose.

---

### 14. Order Data Meaningfully

**Definition**: Arrange data in sequences that reveal patterns, rather than arbitrary orderings like alphabetical.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "There are nearly always better sequences than alphabetical — for example, ordering by content or by data values" (p. 178)

**Examples from text**:
- Reordering Group A/B/C by data values (46%, 53%, 57%) rather than alphabetically
- The voting supertable organized into "topical paragraphs" with horizontal rules dividing groups (p. 179)

**Framework category**: **Distributional** / **Comparative** — Ordering reveals distribution shape and enables comparison.

---

### 15. Integrate Words, Numbers, and Drawing

**Definition**: Combine multiple modes of representation within a single display for richer communication.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Attractive displays "use words, numbers, and drawing together" (p. 177)

**Examples from text**:
- Consumer Reports table integrating text labels, year numbers, and filled/empty circles (p. 174)
- The voting supertable combining category labels, percentages, and visual grouping (p. 179)
- Minard's maps combining geographic drawing with flow-lines and text annotations

**Framework category**: **Instructional** or new category **Multimodal Integration** — The intent is to leverage multiple representational modes.

---

### 16. Display Accessible Complexity

**Definition**: Present detailed, complex information in a way that remains comprehensible and navigable.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Attractive displays "display an accessible complexity of detail" (p. 177)

**Examples from text**:
- The 410-number voting supertable designed to be "organized, sequential" with "reference-like quality" (p. 179)
- Consumer Reports grid making complex multi-dimensional comparison tractable

**Framework category**: **Aggregative** — Accessible complexity is aggregation done well.

---

### 17. Scale Data Measures to Data Volume

**Definition**: Adjust the visual size of data marks as data volume increases — smaller dots for dense scatters, thinner lines for busy time-series.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "As the volume of data increases, data measures must shrink (smaller dots for scatters, thinner lines for busy time-series)" (p. 168)

**Examples from text**:
- Implicit in the discussion of high-information graphics requiring special design care
- The galaxy map using tiny marks to represent 2+ million encoded rectangles

**Framework category**: This is a design technique rather than a communicative intent per se.

## Intent Source Categorization

### From Explicit Prose Statements

These intents were directly articulated in Tufte's prose:

1. **Maximize Data Density** — "Maximize data density and the size of the data matrix, within reason"
2. **Enable Within-View Comparison** — "displaying much information within the view of the eye"
3. **Establish Credibility Through Data Richness** — "Data-rich designs give a context and credibility"
4. **Shrink Graphics** — "Graphics can be shrunk way down"
5. **Show Change Over an Index Variable** — "a series of graphics... indexed by changes in another variable"
6. **Maintain Design Constancy** — "design remains constant through all the frames"
7. **Reveal Interaction Effects** — "revealing interaction or multiplicative effects"
8. **Create Narrative Through Sequence** — "often narrative in content"
9. **Achieve Elegance** — "simplicity of design and complexity of data"
10. **Engage Significant Subject Matter** — "the best graphics are about life and death"
11. **Facilitate Comparison Through Format Choice** — "sentence is a poor way to show more than two numbers"
12. **Order Data Meaningfully** — "better sequences than alphabetical"
13. **Integrate Words, Numbers, and Drawing** — explicit in list of attractive display qualities
14. **Display Accessible Complexity** — explicit in list of attractive display qualities

### From Visual Examples (Seen in Figures)

These intents were demonstrated through the visual examples:

15. **Match Visual Metaphor to Data Meaning** — Sunshine record reversal (light = sun)
16. **Perform Data Graphical Arithmetic** — Melanoma distribution decomposition
17. **Maintain Spatial Orientation** — LA pollution maps preserve geographic shape across frames
18. **Show Cohort Tracking** — Herring age distributions tracking 1904 cohort
19. **Enable Multi-Dimensional Lookup** — Consumer Reports grid enabling car/year/trouble-spot lookup
20. **Show Cross-Species Similarity** — Chromosome comparison layout

### Discoveries from Visual Examination Not Named in Text

These intents emerged from examining the figures but were not explicitly named by Tufte:

- **Color Temperature as Semantic Signal** — The LA pollution maps use a heat-map color scale (blue to red) where color temperature semantically maps to pollution intensity. The choice of "hot colors for high values" is visible but not discussed.

- **Facial Recognition as Spatial Scaffold** — The melanoma distributions use a human head outline, leveraging viewers' automatic facial recognition and spatial memory of head anatomy. This use of familiar anatomy as a reference frame is demonstrated visually but not theorized.

- **Vertical Stacking for Temporal Sequence** — The herring age distributions stack vertically with years ascending, creating an implicit reading order that maps to temporal progression. This compositional choice guides the eye through time.

- **Grid as Organizational Logic** — The Consumer Reports table uses a uniform grid where position (row, column) encodes (trouble-spot, model-year). This Bertin-style "image" construction is visible but the theoretical basis is only referenced in citation.

- **Symbol Gradation for Ordinal Data** — Consumer Reports uses five symbols (empty circle to filled circle) for ordinal quality levels. The visual progression from light to dark encodes the quality ordering — a use of visual weight for semantic weight.

- **Transparency for Layering** — Minard's Hannibal map uses transparent color for flow-lines so underlying geography shows through. This visual layering technique allows information at two levels to coexist.

## Notes on Chapter 8-9 Themes

### The Density Imperative

Chapter 8 makes the strongest case in the entire book for high data density. The quantitative comparison of publications (Nature vs. Pravda; scientific journals vs. newspapers) implies that data density is a measure of graphical seriousness and evidential commitment. The unstated corollary: low-density graphics signal either trivial data or editorial fear of audience comprehension.

### Small Multiples as Fundamental Form

The small multiple emerges as perhaps the most important composite form in Tufte's repertoire. It solves multiple problems simultaneously:
- Comparison across a dimension (the indexing variable)
- High data density (multiple graphics in one view)
- Narrative structure (sequence implies story)
- Interaction detection (shifts in relationships become visible)

The key insight is that small multiples work by holding design constant so only data varies.

### The Sentence-Table-Graphic Continuum

Chapter 9 opens with a practical taxonomy of presentation forms. The key insight is that format choice should follow from the comparison requirements of the data, not from habit or convention. The text-table is positioned as an underused middle ground between prose and full graphics.

### Aesthetics as Ethics

The claim that "the best graphics are about life and death" is not merely aesthetic preference but ethical positioning. Graphical excellence is presented as an intellectual and moral obligation when the subject matter warrants it.

### The Two "Less" Aphorisms

The closing aphorisms crystallize the entire theory:
- "For non-data-ink, less is more" (Mies van der Rohe)
- "For data-ink, less is a bore" (Venturi via Tufte)

This distinguishes between design apparatus (minimize) and data content (maximize) — a distinction that runs through all the extracted intents.
