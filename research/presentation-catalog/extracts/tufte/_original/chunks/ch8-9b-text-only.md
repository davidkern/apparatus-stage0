## Extracted Communicative Intents

### 1. Data-Text Integration

**Name:** Integrate
**Definition:** Unify textual explanation with graphical or tabular presentation so viewers do not need to dart between separated elements.

**How Tufte articulates it:**
> "Words and pictures belong together. Viewers need the help that words can provide. Words on graphics are data-ink, making effective use of the space freed up by erasing redundant and non-data-ink."

> "Data graphics are paragraphs about data and should be treated as such."

> "Words, graphics, and tables are different mechanisms with but a single purpose—the presentation of information."

**Examples referenced:**
- Economic forecasting comparison table (garbled in OCR)
- Leonardo da Vinci's manuscripts showing "gentle but thorough integration of text and figure"
- Newton's scientific manuscripts

**Framework category:** Explanation (with structural/compositional dimension)

**Source type:** Explicit prose

**Notes:** This is a meta-intent about presentation strategy. It suggests that the separation of figures, tables, and text is an artifact of production technology, not a requirement of communication. Biderman is quoted on how graphics became "segregated from text and table as printing technology developed."

### 2. Accessibility via Explanation

**Name:** Annotate-for-Access
**Definition:** Provide on-graphic annotations and labels that explain data directly, removing the need for external legend consultation.

**How Tufte articulates it:**
> "It is nearly always helpful to write little messages on the plotting field to explain the data, to label outliers and interesting data points, to write equations and sometimes tables on the graphic itself, and to integrate the caption and legend into the design."

**Examples referenced:**
- Contrasting "friendly" vs "unfriendly" graphics (tabular comparison provided)

**Framework category:** Explanation (Definition, Example as sub-intents)

**Source type:** Explicit prose

**Notes:** Tufte's "friendly data graphic" section provides a detailed contrast table. Key attributes of friendly graphics include:
- Words spelled out (no mysterious abbreviations)
- Words run left-to-right
- Little messages help explain data
- Labels placed on graphic itself (no external legend)
- Type is clear, modest, upper-and-lower case with serifs

### 3. Invite Exploration

**Name:** Provoke-Curiosity
**Definition:** Design graphics that attract viewers and provoke curiosity rather than repel with visual clutter.

**How Tufte articulates it:**
> "Friendly: graphic attracts viewer, provokes curiosity"
> "Unfriendly: graphic is repellent, filled with chartjunk"

**Examples referenced:**
- Implicit contrast between well-designed and chartjunk-filled graphics

**Framework category:** Emphasis (engagement/attention)

**Source type:** Explicit (from friendly/unfriendly table)

**Notes:** This intent addresses the initial moment of engagement. A graphic should invite rather than repel attention.

### 4. Accommodate Viewer Diversity

**Name:** Design-for-Accessibility
**Definition:** Ensure graphics remain comprehensible to viewers with perceptual differences (color deficiency, etc.).

**How Tufte articulates it:**
> "Friendly: colors, if used, are chosen so that the color-deficient and color-blind (5 to 10 percent of viewers) can make sense of the graphic (blue can be distinguished from other colors by most color-deficient people)"
> "Unfriendly: design insensitive to color-deficient viewers; red and green used for essential contrasts"

**Examples referenced:**
- General principle without specific figure

**Framework category:** Emphasis (accessibility/inclusion)

**Source type:** Explicit prose

**Notes:** Early articulation of inclusive design principles for data graphics.

---

### 5. Visual Balance and Proportion

**Name:** Harmonize-Elements
**Definition:** Achieve visual coherence through balanced proportions between graphical elements (line weights, lettering, spacing).

**How Tufte articulates it:**
> "Graphical elements look better together when their relative proportions are in balance. An integrated quality, an appropriate visual linkage between the various elements, results."

**Examples referenced:**
- Stockhausen's musical score for "Zyklus fur einen Schlagzeuger" (1964) - showing visual balance
- Burgoyne Diller painting with orthogonal lines of differing weights
- Contrast with "heavy handed" Seats/Votes graphic with "clotted ink, poor style of lettering"

**Framework category:** Comparative (balance/harmony) or meta-compositional

**Source type:** Explicit prose

**Notes:** This is both aesthetic principle and communicative strategy. The point is that visual disharmony interferes with data communication.

---

### 6. Differentiate by Meaning

**Name:** Weight-by-Importance
**Definition:** Use visual weight (line thickness, etc.) to represent semantic importance—give greater visual weight to data measures, lesser to structural elements.

**How Tufte articulates it:**
> "The contrast in line weight represents contrast in meaning. The greater meaning is given to the greater line weight; thus the data line should receive greater weight than the connecting verticals."

> "The logic here is a restatement, in different language, of the principle of data-ink maximization."

**Examples referenced:**
- Time-series example showing data line with greater weight than grid
- Diller painting with perpendicular lines of differing weights

**Framework category:** Emphasis

**Source type:** Explicit prose

**Notes:** Maps visual encoding principles to semantic hierarchy.

---

### 7. Orient Horizontally

**Name:** Favor-Horizontal
**Definition:** Design graphics wider than tall to leverage natural eye movement and facilitate labeling.

**How Tufte articulates it:**
Several justifications provided:
1. "Analogy to the horizon. Our eye is naturally practiced in detecting deviations from the horizon."
2. "Ease of labeling. It is easier to write and to read words that read from left to right on a horizontally stretched plotting field."
3. "Emphasis on causal influence... a longer horizontal helps to elaborate the workings of the causal variable in more detail."
4. Tukey's counsel: "Such plots are rather more often better made wider than tall. Wider-than-tall shapes usually make it easier for the eye to follow from left to right."
5. "Playfair's example. Of the 89 graphics in six different books by William Playfair, most (92 percent) are wider than tall."

**Examples referenced:**
- Plot showing dimensions of all 89 Playfair graphics
- Golden Rectangle discussion (1.618 ratio)
- Playfair's preferred proportions (1.4 to 1.8)

**Framework category:** Temporal (for time-series) / compositional principle

**Source type:** Explicit prose with empirical support

**Notes:** The recommendation is "move toward horizontal graphics about 50 percent wider than tall" unless data suggests otherwise. Exception noted: "smoothly-changing curves can stand being taller than wide, but a wiggly curve needs to be wider than tall."

### 8. Guide vs. Prescribe Attention

**Name:** Distinguish-Exploration-from-Communication
**Definition:** Design differently for exploratory analysis (guide how to read) versus settled communication (guide what to read).

**How Tufte articulates it:**
> "A caveat: the use of words and pictures together requires a special sensitivity to the purpose of the design—in particular, whether the graphic is primarily for communication and illustration of a settled finding or, in contrast, for the exploration of a data set."

> "For graphics in exploratory data analysis, words should tell the viewer how to read the design (if it is a technically complex arrangement) and not what to read in terms of content."

**Examples referenced:**
- Reference to visual perception experiments (Gould, "Looking at Pictures")

**Framework category:** Meta-intent (mode selection)

**Source type:** Explicit prose

**Notes:** This distinguishes between two fundamental graphic modes: confirmatory/communicative vs. exploratory. The level of annotation directiveness should match the purpose.

---

### 9. Reveal Complexity

**Name:** Reveal-Complexity
**Definition:** Give visual access to the subtle and difficult—the revelation of complex patterns that would otherwise remain hidden.

**How Tufte articulates it (Epilogue):**
> "Design is choice. The theory of the visual display of quantitative information consists of principles that generate design options and that guide choices among options."

> "What is to be sought in designs for the display of information is the clear portrayal of complexity. Not the complication of the simple; rather the task of the designer is to give visual access to the subtle and the difficult—that is, the revelation of the complex."

**Examples referenced:**
- None specific; capstone statement

**Framework category:** Explanation / Aggregative (summarizing complex phenomena)

**Source type:** Explicit prose (epilogue)

**Notes:** This is Tufte's culminating statement of purpose for data graphics. It distinguishes "complication of the simple" (chartjunk, overdesign) from "revelation of the complex" (genuine insight). This is perhaps the meta-intent underlying all visualization.

---

### 10. Respect Viewer Skepticism

**Name:** Principle-with-Humility
**Definition:** Apply design principles thoughtfully rather than rigidly, recognizing that word authority can distort vision.

**How Tufte articulates it:**
> "The principles should not be applied rigidly or in a peevish spirit; they are not logically or mathematically certain; and it is better to violate any principle than to place graceless or inelegant marks on paper."

> "Most principles of design should be greeted with some skepticism, for word authority can dominate our vision, and we may come to see only through the lenses of word authority rather than with our own eyes."

**Examples referenced:**
- None specific

**Framework category:** Meta-intent (guidance on applying other intents)

**Source type:** Explicit prose (epilogue)

**Notes:** This is a second-order intent about how to apply all other principles. It warns against dogmatism and emphasizes judgment over rule-following.
