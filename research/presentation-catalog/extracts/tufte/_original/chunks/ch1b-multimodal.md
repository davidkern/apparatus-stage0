# Intent Extraction: Tufte Chapter 1b (pp. 33-52)

Source: Edward Tufte, "The Visual Display of Quantitative Information," Chapter 1: Graphical Excellence, pages 33-52.

---

## Extracted Communicative Intents

### 1. Compare Proportions Over Time

**Definition**: Communicate how the relative values of two or more variables change in relation to each other across temporal periods.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Playfair's chart showing wheat prices versus wages over 250 years explicitly compares "the proportion between them and the price of wheat at every different period" to answer "whether the price of wheat had increased relative to wages."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Playfair's wheat price vs. wages chart (1565-1821) with parallel time-series
- The chart showing three parallel series: prices, wages, and reigns of British monarchs

**Framework category**: Comparative + Temporal

---

### 2. Reveal Causal Mechanism

**Definition**: Demonstrate that one variable causes or drives changes in another, moving beyond mere correlation or temporal sequence.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "The problem with time-series is that the simple passage of time is not a good explanatory variable: descriptive chronology is not causal explanation." He notes exceptions "especially when there is a clear mechanism that drives the Y-variable."

**Examples from these pages**:
- U.S. House of Representatives mail volume peaking before elections (mechanism: incumbents exploiting free mailing privileges)
- The accompanying news article provides the causal story the graphic proves

**Framework category**: Evidential (new sub-type: Causal Evidence)

---

### 3. Decompose Signal from Noise

**Definition**: Separate complex data into constituent components to reveal underlying patterns hidden by fluctuations.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "This decomposition of economic data... breaks out the top series into seasonal and trading-day fluctuations (which dominate short-term changes) to reveal the long-run trend adjusted for inflation."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Total Retail Sales decomposition graphic showing: unadjusted data, holiday variation, trading day variation, seasonal variation, irregular component, trend-cycle component, and deflated series
- Note: "The next step would be to bring in additional variables to explain the transformed and improved series at the bottom"

**Framework category**: Aggregative (new sub-type: Decomposition)

---

### 4. Show Before-After State Change

**Definition**: Visually represent the state of a system before and after a discrete event to emphasize the magnitude and nature of transformation.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Finally, a vivid design (with appropriate data) is the before-after time-series."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Cabrera's monopole signal: the jump in magnetic flux showing a candidate magnetic monopole detection
- Minard's bridge collapse on the Rhone (1840): architectural drawing showing the bridge structure before and after collapse

**Framework category**: Comparative + Temporal (specific form: Before-After)

---

### 5. Narrate Through Space and Time

**Definition**: Tell a story by integrating spatial movement, temporal progression, and quantitative variables into a unified visual narrative.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "An especially effective device for enhancing the explanatory power of time-series displays is to add spatial dimensions to the design of the graphic, so that the data are moving over space (in two or three dimensions) as well as over time."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Minard's Napoleon's Russian Campaign map: "Six variables are plotted: the size of the army, its location on a two-dimensional surface, direction of the army's movement, and temperature on various dates during the retreat from Moscow"
- Tufte calls this "may well be the best statistical graphic ever drawn"

**Framework category**: Temporal + Relational (new composite: Narrative)

---

### 6. Show Multivariate Complexity Unobtrusively

**Definition**: Integrate multiple variables into a single graphic without overwhelming the viewer or drawing attention to the technique itself.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "multivariate complexity can be subtly integrated into graphical architecture, integrated so gently and unobtrusively that viewers are hardly aware that they are looking into a world of four or five dimensions. Occasionally graphics are belligerently multivariate, advertising the technique rather than the data. But not these three."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Minard's Napoleon map (6 variables)
- LA air pollution small multiples (3 pollutants x 4 times x 2D space = ~28,800 readings)
- Japanese beetle life cycle diagram

**Framework category**: Relational (design principle rather than distinct intent)

---

### 7. Repeat Design for Comparison (Small Multiples)

**Definition**: Use consistent graphical structure across multiple instances to enable comparison of data changes while holding design constant.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "The air pollution display is a small multiple. The same graphical design structure is repeated for each of the twelve slices or multiples. Small multiples are economical: once viewers understand the design of one slice, they have immediate access to the data in all the other slices. Thus, as the eye moves from one slice to the next, the constancy of the design allows the viewer to focus on changes in the data rather than on changes in graphical design."

**Examples from these pages**:
- LA air pollution grid (3 pollutants x 4 time periods)
- Phillips curve plots for nine countries showing inflation vs. unemployment
- Marey's horse gait sequences

**Framework category**: Comparative (specific form: Small Multiples)

---

### 8. Visualize Movement/Process Over Time

**Definition**: Show how physical movement or biological processes unfold sequentially, capturing dynamics rather than static states.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Demonstrated through Marey's work rather than explicit statement. The text notes these are "time-series forerunner[s]."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Marey's horse gait diagrams (walk, amble, jog-trot, gallop)
- Starfish turning over sequence
- Sea-horse dorsal fin undulations
- Gecko advancement
- Man in black velvet (stick-figure motion capture)
- Japanese beetle life cycle (time x depth below ground)

**Framework category**: Temporal (specific form: Process/Motion Sequence)

---

### 9. Relate Abstract Variables (Relational Graphics)

**Definition**: Plot any variable against any other variable to reveal relationships, freed from geographic or temporal constraints.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "The invention of data graphics required replacing the latitude-longitude coordinates of the map with more abstract measures not based on geographical analogy... any variable quantity could be placed in relationship to any other variable quantity, measured for the same units of observation."

Lambert's statement (1765): "We have in general two variable quantities, x, y, which will be collated with one another by observation..."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Lung cancer death rate vs. cigarette consumption by country
- Temperature vs. thermal conductivity of copper
- Lambert's evaporation rate vs. temperature derivation

**Framework category**: Relational

---

### 10. Confront Theory with Evidence

**Definition**: Use graphical display to test whether theoretical predictions match empirical observations.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "The relational graphic... links at least two variables, encouraging and even imploring the viewer to assess the possible causal relationship between the plotted variables. It confronts causal theories that X causes Y with empirical evidence as to the actual relationship between X and Y."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Smoking and lung cancer scatterplot (tests causal theory)
- Phillips curve plots showing "the collapse of what was once thought to be an inverse relationship between the variables"
- Thermal conductivity of copper: "Theory and measured observations diverge"

**Framework category**: Evidential

---

### 11. Synthesize Literature/Multiple Sources

**Definition**: Aggregate findings from multiple studies or observations into a single graphic to reveal consensus, variation, or disagreement.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Note how effectively the graphic organizes a vast amount of data, recording findings of hundreds of studies on a single page and, at the same time, enforcing comparisons of the varying results."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Thermal conductivity of copper showing measurements from many laboratories, with "connected points are from a single publication, cited by an identification number"
- Divergence attributed to "impurities in the samples of copper"

**Framework category**: Aggregative + Evidential (new sub-type: Meta-analysis/Literature Synthesis)

---

### 12. Show Multivariate Interaction Effects

**Definition**: Visualize how multiple variables interact to produce outcomes, using the data points themselves as information carriers.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "two relational designs of a different sort--wherein the data points are themselves data. Here the effect of two variables interacting is portrayed..."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Lorenz's dog faces showing fear x rage interaction (facial expressions as data points)
- White pine seedlings at different calcium concentrations (actual seedling images as data)

**Framework category**: Relational (specific form: Data-as-Glyph)

---

### 13. Maximize Information Density

**Definition**: Communicate the greatest number of ideas in the shortest time with the least ink in the smallest space.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Graphical excellence is that which gives to the viewer the greatest number of ideas in the shortest time with the least ink in the smallest space."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Minard's Napoleon map (rich story, small poster)
- Total retail sales decomposition (1,296 numbers)
- Air pollution display (28,800 readings)

**Framework category**: Design principle (applies across all categories)

---

### 14. Communicate Complex Ideas Clearly

**Definition**: Present sophisticated multivariate or multidimensional information with clarity, precision, and efficiency.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Graphical excellence consists of complex ideas communicated with clarity, precision, and efficiency."

**Examples from these pages**:
- All exemplary graphics demonstrate this principle

**Framework category**: Design principle (meta-intent)

---

### 15. Tell Truth About Data

**Definition**: Represent data honestly without distortion, acknowledging that graphical integrity is a fundamental requirement.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "And graphical excellence requires telling the truth about the data."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Implied by all positive examples
- Noted design defect: "the vertical grid conceals the height of the December peaks"

**Framework category**: Evidential (design constraint)

---

### 16. Convey Historical Continuity

**Definition**: Show how events, reigns, or periods connect across long time spans to give "a sense of the march of history."

**How Tufte articulates it**: "This superb construction of E. J. Marey brings together several sets of facts about English rulers into a time-series that conveys a sense of the march of history."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Marey's English rulers chart showing reigns, periods of peace and war
- Playfair's wheat/wages chart with monarch reigns as third series

**Framework category**: Temporal (specific form: Historical Timeline)

---

### 17. Aid Memory and Retention

**Definition**: Present information in visual form specifically to enhance recall and understanding.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Playfair wrote that his mode "was generally approved of as not only facilitating, but rendering those studies more clear, and retained more easily by the memory."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Stated as general advantage of graphical method

**Framework category**: Instructional (cognitive purpose)

---

### 18. Enable Size Comparison Through Similar Forms

**Definition**: Use consistent visual forms to allow accurate comparison of quantities that would otherwise be difficult to compare.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Playfair: "The present charts are in like manner intended to aid statistical studies, by shewing to the eye the sizes of different countries represented by similar forms, for where forms are not similar, the eye cannot compare them easily nor accurately."

**Examples from these pages**:
- Statistical Breviary plate showing countries as circles (area = land area), with lines for population and revenue

**Framework category**: Comparative + Distributional

## Anti-Patterns Revealing Purposes by Negation

### A1. Non-comparative, Highly Labeled Small Data Sets

**Implied intent**: Graphics should enable comparison; small labeled datasets belong in tables.

**Source**: Playfair's skepticism about his bar chart: "it is much inferior in utility... for though it gives the extent of the different branches of trade, it does not compare the same branch of commerce with itself at different periods."

Tufte's endorsement: "He was right: small, noncomparative, highly labeled data sets usually belong in tables."

---

### A2. Belligerent Multivariance

**Implied intent**: Complexity should serve data, not advertise technique.

**Source**: "Occasionally graphics are belligerently multivariate, advertising the technique rather than the data."

---

### A3. Design Defects that Obscure Data

**Implied intent**: Visual elements should reveal, not conceal.

**Source**: "Note a significant defect in the design, however: the vertical grid conceals the height of the December peaks."

---

## Summary: Principles of Graphical Excellence (Tufte's Explicit List, p. 51)

1. "Graphical excellence is the well-designed presentation of interesting data--a matter of substance, of statistics, and of design."

2. "Graphical excellence consists of complex ideas communicated with clarity, precision, and efficiency."

3. "Graphical excellence is that which gives to the viewer the greatest number of ideas in the shortest time with the least ink in the smallest space."

4. "Graphical excellence is nearly always multivariate."

5. "And graphical excellence requires telling the truth about the data."

### Discoveries from Visual Examination Not Named in Text

1. **Gestalt continuity as narrative device**: In the Napoleon map, the continuous flow-line creates a sense of journey that text cannot fully capture. The visual "path" invites the eye to follow the army's movement.

2. **Color coding for categorical distinction**: The tan/black distinction in the Napoleon map (advance vs. retreat) and the cross-hatching vs. solid bars in Playfair's Scotland trade chart use color/pattern to separate categorical meanings - this encoding choice is shown but not explicitly discussed as a principle.

3. **Vertical stacking for component analysis**: The retail sales decomposition stacks components vertically, creating an implicit reading order from raw to refined. This compositional choice for analytical hierarchy is demonstrated but not named.

4. **Ground plane as reference in life-cycle graphics**: The Japanese beetle diagram uses the ground surface as a spatial anchor, with depth below ground as a meaningful axis. This use of physical reference planes for abstract data is visible but not theorized.

5. **Scale disruption for emphasis**: The monopole signal graph uses the dramatic step-change against background noise to create visual emphasis on the detection event. The contrast between steady-state and transition is a visual rhetorical device.

6. **Specimen-as-data-point**: The white pine seedlings and dog faces use actual visual representations as plot points. This "glyph" approach where the data point is itself informationally rich (showing root structure, foliage density, facial expression) is a form not explicitly named but demonstrated.

## Potential New Framework Categories or Sub-types Suggested

1. **Causal Evidence** (under Evidential): Graphics that demonstrate mechanism, not just correlation
2. **Decomposition** (under Aggregative): Breaking complex signals into constituent parts
3. **Narrative** (Temporal + Relational composite): Space-time-story integration
4. **Literature Synthesis** (Aggregative + Evidential): Multi-source evidence aggregation
5. **Process Sequence** (under Temporal): Motion/lifecycle visualization
6. **Data-as-Glyph** (under Relational): Information-rich data point representations
