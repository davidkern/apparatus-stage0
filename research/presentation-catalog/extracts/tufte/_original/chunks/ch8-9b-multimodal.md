# Communicative Intents from Tufte Chapter 9 "Aesthetics and Technique" (pp. 180-190) and Epilogue

Source: Edward Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, Chapter 9 "Aesthetics and Technique" (continued) and Epilogue

---

## Extracted Intents

### 1. Integrate Text and Data (Data/Text Integration)

**Definition**: Treat data graphics as paragraphs about data, seamlessly weaving quantitative information into the textual narrative rather than segregating them.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Data graphics are paragraphs about data and should be treated as such." (p. 181) "Words and pictures belong together. Viewers need the help that words can provide." (p. 180)

**Examples from text**:
- Leonardo da Vinci's manuscripts showing "gentle but thorough integration of text and figure, a quality rarely seen in modern work" (p. 182)
- The "Winners and Losers in the Forecasting Game" graphic (p. 180) that organizes forecaster predictions into a visual table with labels directly embedded
- Critique of "See Fig. 2" segregation where "figures all too often located on the back of the adjacent page" (p. 181)

**Framework category**: **Instructional** / **Contextual** — The graphic exists as part of a communicative whole with text; neither is subordinate.

---

### 2. Label Directly on Graphic (Direct Annotation)

**Definition**: Place identifying labels, explanations, and annotations directly on the graphical surface rather than requiring viewers to decode through separate legends.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "It is nearly always helpful to write little messages on the plotting field to explain the data, to label outliers and interesting data points, to write equations and sometimes tables on the graphic itself, and to integrate the caption and legend into the design" (p. 180)

**Examples from text**:
- The forecasting comparison graphic with institution names placed directly next to their predictions
- Friendly graphics principle: "labels are placed on the graphic itself; no legend is required" vs. unfriendly "obscure codings require going back and forth between legend and graphic" (p. 183)

**Framework category**: **Explanation** — Labels serve the explanatory function of identifying what the viewer is seeing.

---

### 3. Make Complexity Accessible

**Definition**: Give viewers access to rich, complex data through design that welcomes rather than repels, opening up the data rather than obscuring it.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Section title "Making Complexity Accessible: Combining Words, Numbers, and Pictures" (p. 180); "An occasional data graphic displays such care in design that it is particularly accessible and open to the eye, as if the designer had the viewer in mind at every turn" (p. 183)

**Examples from text**:
- The "friendly data graphic" concept: graphics that "attract viewer, provoke curiosity" vs. those that are "repellent, filled with chartjunk" (p. 183)
- Contrast between friendly attributes (words spelled out, left-to-right reading, little messages helping explain) and unfriendly (abbreviations requiring decoding, cryptic, vertical text)

**Framework category**: **Emphasis** — Accessibility is about ensuring the important content reaches the viewer; also new category **Hospitality** or **Viewer-Orientation**.

---

### 4. Minimize Encoding Burden

**Definition**: Reduce the cognitive work required to decode the graphic by avoiding elaborate encoding schemes, cryptic abbreviations, and obscure visual conventions.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Through the friendly/unfriendly contrast: "words are spelled out, mysterious and elaborate encoding avoided" vs. "abbreviations abound, requiring the viewer to sort through text to decode abbreviations" (p. 183)

**Examples from text**:
- Friendly: "elaborately encoded shadings, cross-hatching, and colors are avoided"
- Unfriendly: "obscure codings require going back and forth between legend and graphic"
- Color accessibility: "colors, if used, are chosen so that the color-deficient and color-blind (5 to 10 percent of viewers) can make sense of the graphic" (p. 183)

**Framework category**: **Emphasis** — Reducing encoding burden is a form of ensuring emphasis lands on the data, not the decoding process.

---

### 5. Respect Reading Direction

**Definition**: Orient textual elements to follow natural reading patterns (left-to-right for occidental languages) rather than forcing viewers to read vertically or in multiple directions.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Friendly graphics have "words run from left to right, the usual direction for reading occidental languages" vs. unfriendly where "words run vertically, particularly along the Y-axis; words run in several different directions" (p. 183)

**Examples from text**:
- Horizontal labeling diagram (p. 187) showing "some labels" and "some other labels" written left-to-right vs. vertical stacked labels
- The rationale: "It is easier to write and to read words that read from left to right on a horizontally stretched plotting-field" (p. 187)

**Framework category**: **Instruction** — Reading direction is about guiding the viewer through the graphic in a comprehensible sequence.

---

### 6. Guide Attention Appropriately

**Definition**: Direct viewer attention through words and annotation to support the graphic's purpose, whether for exploration (how to read) or communication (what to read).

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Words on and around graphics are highly effective—sometimes all too effective—in telling viewers how to allocate their attention to the various parts of the data display. Thus, for graphics in exploratory data analysis, words should tell the viewer how to read the design... and not what to read in terms of content." (p. 182)

**Examples from text**:
- The caveat about exploration vs. communication: graphics for settled findings can direct interpretation, but exploratory graphics should explain the reading method without prejudging conclusions
- Reference to visual perception research: "word instructions substantially determine eye movements in viewing pictures" (p. 182, citing Gould)

**Framework category**: **Instruction** — Guiding attention is fundamentally instructional; also **Emphasis** for settled findings.

---

### 7. Maintain Visual Proportion and Balance

**Definition**: Create harmonious relationships among graphical elements (lines, text, shading) so they form a visually coherent whole.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Graphical elements look better together when their relative proportions are in balance. An integrated quality, an appropriate visual linkage between the various elements, results." (p. 184)

**Examples from text**:
- Stockhausen's musical score "Zyklus" as positive example of visual balance (p. 184)
- Counter-example: "Seats and Votes in 1968" graph described as "heavy handed, with nearly every element out of balance: the clotted ink, the poor style of lettering, the puffed-up display of a small data set, the coarse texture of the entire graphic, and the mismatch between drawing and surrounding text" (p. 184)

**Framework category**: New category: **Aesthetic Coherence** — The graphic functions as a unified visual composition.

---

### 8. Use Thin Lines (Minimize Line Weight)

**Definition**: Employ thin, light lines in data graphics to create elegance and avoid visual heaviness.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Lines in data graphics should be thin. One reason eighteenth- and nineteenth-century graphics look so good is that they were engraved on copper plates, with a characteristic hair-thin line. The drafting pens of twentieth-century mechanical drawing thickened linework, making it clumsy and unattractive." (p. 185)

**Examples from text**:
- Copper plate engravings of 18th-19th century as positive models
- Contrast with 20th century mechanical drafting pen graphics

**Framework category**: **Aesthetic Coherence** — Line weight is a formal property affecting overall visual quality.

---

### 9. Differentiate by Line Weight (Visual Hierarchy through Weight)

**Definition**: Use contrasting line weights to encode different types of information, with heavier lines for data and lighter lines for structural elements.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "An effective aesthetic device is the orthogonal intersection of lines of different weights... The contrast in line weight represents contrast in meaning. The greater meaning is given to the greater line weight; thus the data line should receive greater weight than the connecting verticals." (pp. 185-186)

**Examples from text**:
- Mondrian/Diller painting showing orthogonal intersection of different weight lines (p. 185)
- Time-series diagram showing heavier data line vs. lighter connecting verticals (p. 186)
- The logic: "a restatement, in different language, of the principle of data-ink maximization" (p. 186)

**Framework category**: **Emphasis** — Line weight creates visual hierarchy that emphasizes data over structure.

---

### 10. Favor Horizontal Orientation

**Definition**: Design graphics to extend horizontally (wider than tall) to align with natural reading patterns, horizon perception, and causal reasoning conventions.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Graphics should tend toward the horizontal, greater in length than height" (p. 186)

**Examples from text**:
- Four arguments: (1) "analogy to the horizon. Our eye is naturally practiced in detecting deviations from the horizon" (p. 186); (2) "ease of labeling" for left-to-right reading (p. 187); (3) "emphasis on causal influence" with cause on horizontal axis (p. 187); (4) Tukey's counsel that "wiggly curve needs to be wider than tall" (p. 188)
- Playfair's practice: "Of the 89 graphics in six different books by William Playfair, most (92 percent) are wider than tall" (p. 188)

**Framework category**: **Temporal** / **Relational** — Horizontal orientation supports time-series and causal displays; also **Aesthetic Coherence**.

---

### 11. Detect Deviation from Baseline

**Definition**: Enable perception of deviations from a reference line or baseline, particularly in time-series displays.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Through the horizon analogy and shading suggestion.

**Examples from text**:
- "Our eye is naturally practiced in detecting deviations from the horizon, and graphic design should take advantage of this fact. Horizontally stretched time-series are more accessible to the eye" (p. 186)
- Shaded area chart showing filled region below time-series line: "a shaded, high contrast display might occasionally be better than the floating snake" (p. 187)

**Framework category**: **Comparative** — Deviation detection is comparison against a baseline reference.

---

### 12. Let Data Shape Determine Graphic Shape

**Definition**: Allow the nature of the data itself to suggest the proportions of the graphic rather than imposing arbitrary aspect ratios.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "If the nature of the data suggests the shape of the graphic, follow that suggestion." (p. 190)

**Examples from text**:
- Playfair's exceptions to horizontal orientation: "skyrocketing government debt displays" that warranted vertical proportion (p. 188)
- Golden Rectangle discussion noting that context-dependence matters: "viewer responses were found to be highly context-dependent" (p. 190)

**Framework category**: **Evidential** — Data integrity includes letting data characteristics drive form.

---

### 13. Elaborate Causal Variable

**Definition**: Provide detailed resolution on the independent/causal variable (typically on horizontal axis) to show its workings in detail.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Many graphics plot, in essence, effect [on y-axis] vs. cause [on x-axis] and a longer horizontal helps to elaborate the workings of the causal variable in more detail." (p. 187)

**Examples from text**:
- The cause-effect diagram (p. 187) showing horizontal axis labeled "cause" and vertical labeled "effect"

**Framework category**: **Relational** — Causal elaboration shows the relationship between cause and effect more completely.

---

### 14. Reveal Complexity (Not Complicate Simplicity)

**Definition**: Use graphics to make complex data comprehensible rather than to inflate simple data into appearing complex.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "What is to be sought in designs for the display of information is the clear portrayal of complexity. Not the complication of the simple; rather the task of the designer is to give visual access to the subtle and the difficult—that is, the revelation of the complex." (Epilogue)

**Examples from text**:
- This is the culminating statement of the entire book
- The contrast with "puffed-up display of a small data set" (p. 184) as anti-example

**Framework category**: **Evidential** — Revealing complexity is about truthful representation of data's actual richness.

---

### 15. Apply Principles Flexibly (Design as Choice)

**Definition**: Exercise judgment in applying design principles, recognizing that no principle is absolute and graceful execution matters more than rigid rule-following.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Design is choice. The theory of the visual display of quantitative information consists of principles that generate design options and that guide choices among options. The principles should not be applied rigidly or in a peevish spirit; they are not logically or mathematically certain; and it is better to violate any principle than to place graceless or inelegant marks on paper." (Epilogue)

**Examples from text**:
- Warning against "word authority" dominating vision: "we may come to see only through the lenses of word authority rather than with our own eyes"
- The overall pragmatic tone of the chapter with "if... otherwise" formulations

**Framework category**: This is a meta-principle about the application of all intents rather than an intent itself.

---

### 16. Design for Typography Legibility

**Definition**: Use typographic conventions that maximize readability: upper-and-lower case, serif fonts, modest sizing.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Through the friendly/unfriendly contrast and Josef Albers quotation: "the more the letters are differentiated from each other, the easier is the reading... words consisting of only capital letters present the most difficult reading—because of their equal height, equal volume, and, with most, their equal width." (p. 183)

**Examples from text**:
- Friendly: "type is clear, precise, modest; lettering may be done by hand" and "type is upper-and-lower case, with serifs"
- Unfriendly: "type is clotted, overbearing" and "type is all capitals, sans serif"

**Framework category**: **Emphasis** — Typography affects what gets emphasized and how easily content is absorbed.

---

### 17. Design for Inclusive Accessibility

**Definition**: Ensure graphics can be understood by viewers with visual differences, including color-deficiency and color-blindness.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "colors, if used, are chosen so that the color-deficient and color-blind (5 to 10 percent of viewers) can make sense of the graphic (blue can be distinguished from other colors by most color-deficient people)" vs. "design insensitive to color-deficient viewers; red and green used for essential contrasts" (p. 183)

**Examples from text**:
- The specific guidance about blue being distinguishable
- The 5-10% statistic establishing this as a significant design consideration

**Framework category**: **Emphasis** / New category: **Accessibility** — Ensuring content reaches all viewers.

### Visual-Only Discoveries (Not Named in Text)

These intents emerged from examining figures but were not explicitly theorized by Tufte:

- **Visual Rhythm through Repetition** — The Stockhausen musical score demonstrates how repeated visual elements at varying intervals create rhythm. This compositional principle is shown but not discussed as a data graphics technique.

- **Negative Space as Structural Element** — The Mondrian/Diller composition uses white space as actively as black lines. The comparison between original (varying line weights) and uniform version shows how proportional negative space contributes to composition.

- **Aspect Ratio Encoding Meaning** — The Golden Rectangle discussion and Playfair's exceptions (tall debt displays) suggest aspect ratio itself can carry semantic content—a skyrocketing quantity naturally suggests vertical extension. This is implied but not articulated as a encoding principle.

- **Self-Demonstrating Graphics** — The scatter plot of Playfair's graphic dimensions (p. 188) is itself a horizontal graphic demonstrating horizontal orientation, with labeled zones ("Graphic is taller than wide," "Graphic is square," "Graphic is wider than tall"). This self-referential quality is shown but not remarked upon.

## Notes on Tufte's Aesthetic Philosophy

This section of Tufte reveals several overarching aesthetic commitments:

1. **Integration over Segregation**: Tufte repeatedly argues against artificial separation—of text from graphics, of legend from data, of production technique from information conveyed. The historical observation that "printing technology... moved graphics to a segregated position" (Biderman quotation, p. 181) frames this as a technological degradation to be reversed.

2. **Hospitality to Viewers**: The "friendly data graphic" concept centers the viewer's experience. Design choices should arise from empathy with the person trying to understand the data, not from convention or production convenience.

3. **Historical Exemplars**: Leonardo da Vinci, William Playfair, and 18th-century copper plate engravings serve as models. Tufte argues that certain graphical achievements were lost rather than superseded—thin lines, integrated text/image, proportional balance.

4. **Principled Pragmatism**: The Epilogue's "Design is choice" framing refuses dogmatism. Principles generate options; judgment selects among them. Grace matters more than rule-following.

5. **Complexity as Virtue**: The book's final statement—"the revelation of the complex"—positions complexity as the proper subject of data graphics. Simplification is not the goal; making complexity accessible is.

## Leonardo da Vinci Manuscript (Visual Example)

The reproduction of Leonardo da Vinci's Codex Urbinas Latinus 1270 (p. 182) demonstrates integration of text and figure in Renaissance manuscript tradition. The image shows:

- Geometric diagrams embedded within flowing Italian text
- Text wrapping around visual elements naturally
- Mathematical symbols interspersed with prose
- A unified compositional field where word and image occupy the same visual space

This exemplifies the "gentle but thorough integration" Tufte celebrates as "rarely seen in modern work."

## Friendly vs. Unfriendly Summary (Explicit Contrast)

Tufte's friendly/unfriendly table (p. 183) provides explicit paired contrasts:

| Dimension | Friendly | Unfriendly |
|-----------|----------|------------|
| Encoding | Words spelled out | Abbreviations require decoding |
| Reading direction | Left to right | Vertical, multiple directions |
| Explanation | Little messages help explain | Cryptic, requires scattered text |
| Legend | Labels on graphic, no legend needed | Obscure codings, legend required |
| Emotional response | Attracts, provokes curiosity | Repellent, filled with chartjunk |
| Color | Accessible to color-deficient | Red/green for essential contrasts |
| Typography | Clear, precise, modest, hand-done OK | Clotted, overbearing |
| Case | Upper-and-lower case, with serifs | All capitals, sans serif |

This table itself demonstrates the principle of direct labeling and accessible complexity.
