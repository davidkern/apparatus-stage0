These intents emerged from examining the graphics beyond what the prose explicitly names:

1. **Labeling Directness** - The political science graphic (p. 120) uses abbreviated computer labels ("INF", "UNEM", "SHORT") while the replacement table (p. 121) uses full descriptive text. The visual comparison reveals that graphics should use human-readable labels, but this specific intent is shown rather than named.

2. **Scale Legibility** - The political science bar chart (p. 120) has "overly busy vertical scaling shows more percentage markers and labels than there are actual data points." The visual excess of scale lines reveals an intent about matching scale granularity to data granularity.

3. **Data-Form Matching** - The California Water Atlas (p. 119) embeds waffle charts onto a map. Visually, the waffle chart form seems chosen for aesthetic variety rather than data properties. This suggests an intent about matching visualization form to data type, demonstrated but not explicitly theorized.

4. **Historical Progression Awareness** - The table of chartjunk prevalence over time (p. 112) visually demonstrates that computer graphics have worsened rather than improved chartjunk prevalence (12% in 1914 vs 68% in 1980). This implies an intent about learning from graphical history, but the concept is not named as such.

5. **Practical Efficiency** - The quartile plot discussion notes "The straightedge need only be placed on the paper once" versus "six separate placings for the box plot." This reveals an intent about production efficiency not just perceptual efficiency--ease of drawing matters. This is mentioned briefly but not developed as a formal principle.

6. **Aesthetic Quality as Criterion** - "Nor does it look very good" is used to reject a design variant (p. 124). This elevates aesthetic judgment to co-equal status with data-ink ratio, but the relationship between aesthetics and communication remains implicit.
