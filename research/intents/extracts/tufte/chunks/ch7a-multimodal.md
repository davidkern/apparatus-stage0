# Communicative Intents from Tufte Chapter 7 "Multifunctioning Graphical Elements" (pp. 139-149)

Source: Edward Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, Chapter 7 "Multifunctioning Graphical Elements" (partial)

---

## Extracted Intents

### 1. Maximize Data Density (Graphical Economy)

**Definition**: Encode as much information as possible per unit of graphical element, making each mark serve multiple communicative functions simultaneously.

**How Tufte articulates it**: The chapter's central argument is that graphical elements should "multifunction" — a single mark, line, or symbol should carry multiple data values or serve multiple roles. Tufte notes we reach toward "the limit of graphical economy of presentation, imagination, and, let it be admitted, eccentricity" (p. 142).

**Examples from text**:
- Chernoff faces where each facial feature encodes a different variable while the overall face position encodes two more dimensions (p. 142)
- George Herbert's "Easter Wings" poem where line length encodes quantity (wealth/poverty) while the words convey meaning (p. 143)
- Youden's triangle-shaped text about the normal law of error — the shape itself represents the normal distribution (p. 143)

**Framework category**: **Aggregative** — Multiple data streams compressed into unified visual form.

---

### 2. Enable Multi-Dimensional Comparison Within Single Marks

**Definition**: Allow viewers to compare multiple variables simultaneously by encoding them in different visual features of the same graphical object.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Describes how Chernoff faces let "the analyst observe the standard X-Y scatterplot and then turn to the within-scatter detail, seeking clusters of similar observations over the X-Y plane" (p. 142).

**Examples from text**:
- Chernoff faces scatterplot: position encodes two variables while facial features encode additional dimensions (p. 142)
- The analyst workflow: observe position patterns, then examine facial similarity within clusters

**Framework category**: **Comparative** and **Distributional** — Enables comparison across multiple dimensions simultaneously while showing distributional patterns.

---

### 3. Identify Outliers and Anomalies (Stranger Detection)

**Definition**: Make observations that deviate from local patterns immediately visible by their inconsistency with neighbors.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Outlying faces and those inconsistent with others in the neighborhood—they are, of course, *strangers*—should be identified by observation number or name" (p. 142). The term "stranger" (italicized) emphasizes the human-intuitive nature of anomaly detection.

**Examples from text**:
- The Chernoff faces scatterplot with a labeled "stranger" — a face that differs visually from its spatial neighbors (p. 142)
- The stranger is identified not just by statistical distance but by visual incongruity

**Framework category**: **Emphasis** (subcategory: Warning/Alert) — Drawing attention to observations requiring investigation. Also **Comparative** — the stranger is defined by contrast with neighbors.

---

### 4. Make Form Mirror Content (Iconic Representation)

**Definition**: Design the visual representation so its shape directly depicts the phenomenon being measured, creating immediate semantic connection.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Describes this as a "graphical pun: the visual data as the data measure" (p. 144). The graphic doesn't just represent the data abstractly — it IS a visual instance of what it measures.

**Examples from text**:
- Lane stripe graphic: "Portrayed in the graphic is the actual physical pattern painted on the road" (p. 144). States' standards for lane markings are shown using the actual dash patterns.
- "Easter Wings": The wing shape visually embodies the spiritual rising/falling described in the text (p. 143)
- Youden's normal law text: The triangular shape approximates the bell curve it describes (p. 143)

**Framework category**: New category suggestion: **Iconic/Mimetic** — The form directly represents its referent. Could be subsumed under **Explanation** (Definition) since the graphic defines by showing.

---

### 5. Enable Comparison Through Ordering

**Definition**: Arrange data in meaningful sequences that reveal patterns and enable systematic comparison across categories.

**How Tufte articulates it**: The lane stripe graphic shows "48 U.S. states ordered by the length of the painted mark" (p. 144).

**Examples from text**:
- Lane stripe chart: States ordered from shortest marks (California with gaps) to longest marks (Utah with solid lines), making the progression visible (p. 144)
- The ordering transforms a simple enumeration into a ranked comparison

**Framework category**: **Comparative** and **Distributional** — Ordering reveals distribution while enabling position-based comparison.

---

### 6. Transform Scaffold Into Data (Data-Based Grids)

**Definition**: Make graphical infrastructure (grids, frames, axes) serve as data carriers rather than passive coordinate systems.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Very occasionally the grid can report directly on the data" (p. 145). Tufte argues that "erasing the grid would erase measured data" in well-designed graphics, unlike conventional grids where erasure loses only reference marks.

**Examples from text**:
- Magnetohydrodynamic calculation grid: "the grid is formed by the location of measurement instruments; the plain dots register a zero reading" (p. 145)
- Galton's height inheritance table: "The arrangement of data in this table-graphic yields an internal grid, a rare example of data as grid" (p. 145)
- Mitchell Map comparison: "Here the grid is the element of interest, rather than the map" — grid distortion shows cartographic error (p. 146)

**Framework category**: **Comparative** (in Mitchell map case) and **Distributional** — The grid itself communicates distributional or measurement information.

---

### 7. Reveal Systematic Error or Distortion

**Definition**: Make visible the degree and pattern of deviation between a representation and ground truth.

**How Tufte articulates it**: The Mitchell Map comparison shows "the relative stretching, compressing, and twisting of the earth surface" to reveal historical cartographic error (p. 146).

**Examples from text**:
- Mitchell Map paired comparison: Modern accurate map placed alongside 1783 Mitchell map with corresponding labeled grids. "The deformation of earth surface that Mitchell incorporated into his map (from either ignorance or error)" becomes visible through grid comparison (p. 146)

**Framework category**: **Comparative** (Deviation) and **Evidential** — Makes the magnitude and nature of error evident.

---

### 8. Reveal Interaction Effects (Multi-Variable Slopes)

**Definition**: Show how the effect of one variable depends on the level of another variable, making interaction patterns visually apparent.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Describes reading slopes in two directions: "Note there is no reliable slope associated with religion in 1956, once party is controlled; in 1960, a systematic effect is found. Reading the slopes in the other direction shows the persistent effect of party in both elections" (p. 147).

**Examples from text**:
- 3D voting graphics (1956 vs 1960): Party identification on one axis, religion (Protestant to Catholic) on another, Democratic vote percentage on vertical. The surface curvature shows interaction (p. 147)
- The comparison between two time periods reveals when religion mattered (1960 Catholic candidate) vs when it did not (1956)

**Framework category**: **Relational** (Interaction/Moderation) and **Comparative** — Shows how relationships change across conditions.

---

### 9. Annotate Data with Contextual Events

**Definition**: Integrate external events or milestones directly into the data display, connecting quantitative patterns to historical or causal context.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Playfair tied the grid to the data in his skyrocketing debt graphic... the vertical grid lines in the published version are irregularly spaced, keyed to significant events" (p. 148).

**Examples from text**:
- Playfair's British national debt chart: Grid lines mark "Revolution of the Abdication of K William," "Accession of Queen Ann," "Beginning of the Spanish War," "End of Continental War," "Beginning of American War," "End of American War" (p. 148)
- "The data-based grid is a shrewd graphical device, serving rather than fighting with the data" (p. 148)

**Framework category**: **Temporal** and **Relational** (Causation) — Temporal markers suggest causal relationships between events and data patterns.

---

### 10. Show Data Range Precisely (Range-Frames and Range-Labels)

**Definition**: Use axis endpoints to communicate actual data extent rather than arbitrary round numbers, conveying range information through frame position.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "Rather than showing the minimum round number and the maximum round number at the ends of the frame, show the actual minimum and maximum realized in the data" (p. 149). "With its greater precision and two tick-marks less of non-data-ink, the range-frame with range-labels is superior" (p. 149).

**Examples from text**:
- Progression from conventional frame (0-10-20-30-40) to range-frame to range-frame with range-labels (4-10-20-30-37) (p. 149)
- The labels themselves become data by marking actual rather than conventional boundaries

**Framework category**: **Distributional** (Range/Extent) — Communicates the actual spread of data.

---

### 11. Encode Temporal/Ordinal Information in Identifiers

**Definition**: Make identification codes carry ordering or temporal information, allowing viewers to track sequences without additional encoding.

**How Tufte articulates it**: Critiques the tungsten conductivity graphic where "circled numbers fail to double-function" because studies are ordered alphabetically rather than by date. Suggests that numbering by publication date would let viewers "see which of the early studies got the right answer" and track "movement of the studies toward the 'correct' recommended values... This extra information requires no additional ink" (pp. 149-150).

**Examples from text**:
- Thermal conductivity of tungsten graphic: 100+ numbered studies plotted against temperature (p. 150)
- Proposed improvement: Use "1" for earliest study, or "61c" for "third study published in 1961"

**Framework category**: **Temporal** and **Aggregative** — Embeds temporal sequence within identification system.

---

### 12. Reduce Eye Movement (Labels as Data Positions)

**Definition**: Position labels directly at data points to eliminate the cognitive work of shuttling between data and distant axes.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "In most graphics, the coordinate labels are far from the data measures. Consequently the eye of the viewer must move back and forth between the path formed by the data and the coordinate positions arrayed along the margins... Sometimes this eye-work can be eliminated entirely by turning the coordinate labels into data measures" (p. 150).

**Examples from text**:
- JASA histogram transformation: X-axis labels relocated from margin to mark the path of data (p. 151)
- Y-scale replaced by exact probability values positioned at data heights: ".177  0", ".114  1", ".075  2", etc. (p. 151)
- "The Y-scale now resembles the dashes of the dot-dash-plot" (p. 151)

**Framework category**: **Distributional** — Shows marginal distribution through label positioning.

---

### 13. Show Marginal Distributions Through Label Positioning

**Definition**: Use the arrangement of labels to reveal the distribution of data along each axis.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "The vertical column of data-positioned numbers serving as the dashes to indicate the marginal distribution" (p. 151).

**Examples from text**:
- Enhanced dot-dash-plot: Numbers in margin (20.3, 15.2, 14.6, 11.3, 10.1, 8.4, 5.1) on Y-axis and (81, 123, 182, 227, 255, 291, 357) on X-axis show actual data values while their vertical/horizontal positions reveal the marginal distribution (p. 152)
- "The numbers in the margin eliminate the standard frame and even a range-frame, replace the coordinate ticks, show the marginal distribution of both variables, and record the exact values" (p. 152)

**Framework category**: **Distributional** — Multiple distributional intents served by single design choice.

---

### 14. Support Fine-Grained Analysis (Small Data Sets)

**Definition**: Provide detailed individual-level information when the data set is small enough to warrant it.

**How Tufte articulates it**: "This graphical arrangement performs better for smaller data sets (say 30 observations or less) and when a fine level of detail is required" (p. 152).

**Examples from text**:
- The enhanced dot-dash-plot showing exact values for each observation
- Recognition that different data densities call for different design strategies

**Framework category**: **Explanation** (Detail) — Prioritizes individual-observation precision over pattern summarization.

## Intent Source Categorization

### Intents from Explicit Prose

These intents were directly articulated by Tufte in the text:

1. **Maximize Data Density** — "the limit of graphical economy" (p. 142)
2. **Identify Outliers** — "strangers—should be identified" (p. 142)
3. **Make Form Mirror Content** — "graphical pun: the visual data as the data measure" (p. 144)
4. **Transform Scaffold Into Data** — "the grid can report directly on the data" (p. 145)
5. **Annotate Data with Contextual Events** — "grid lines... keyed to significant events" (p. 148)
6. **Show Data Range Precisely** — "show the actual minimum and maximum realized in the data" (p. 149)
7. **Encode Temporal Information in Identifiers** — "This extra information requires no additional ink" (p. 150)
8. **Reduce Eye Movement** — "eye-work can be eliminated entirely" (p. 150)
9. **Support Fine-Grained Analysis** — "performs better for smaller data sets" (p. 152)

### Intents from Visual Examples (with prose support)

These intents are primarily demonstrated through figures, with some textual framing:

1. **Enable Multi-Dimensional Comparison** — Chernoff faces figure with brief description (p. 142)
2. **Enable Comparison Through Ordering** — Lane stripe chart ordered by painted mark length (p. 144)
3. **Reveal Systematic Error** — Mitchell Map comparison (p. 146)
4. **Reveal Interaction Effects** — 1956/1960 voting 3D charts (p. 147)
5. **Show Marginal Distributions** — Dot-dash-plot enhancement (pp. 151-152)

### Visual-Only Discoveries

These intents emerged from examining the figures beyond what text explicitly states:

1. **Stranger as Contextual Anomaly** — The visual shows "A stranger" labeled on a face that differs from spatial neighbors; the text mentions identification but the visual demonstrates that anomaly is defined by local context, not global deviation
2. **Data-Grid as Measurement Coverage** — The magnetohydrodynamic grid (p. 145) visually shows that absence of dots indicates "where no readings were taken" — the grid communicates measurement completeness, not just readings
3. **Playful Presentation Legitimacy** — The Herbert poem and Youden triangle demonstrate that whimsy can serve serious communicative purpose; the visual examples are charming in ways the prose ("eccentricity") only hints at
4. **Temporal Comparison Through Parallel Structure** — The 1956/1960 voting charts placed vertically demonstrate before/after comparison through identical visual structure with different surface shapes; the prose describes the pattern but the power comes from the visual parallel
5. **Recursive Representation** — The Carol Moore "striking design with data-based labels" (p. 152) shows a bar chart where bars are made of lines, creating a visual recursion effect that demonstrates data density in a way that text cannot capture

## Notes on Chapter 7's Contribution to Intent Taxonomy

This chapter introduces a meta-intent not explicit in the framework: **Multifunctioning** itself — the principle that each graphical element should serve multiple communicative purposes simultaneously. This is less an intent category than a design principle that applies across categories.

The chapter also suggests a potential new intent category: **Iconic/Mimetic Representation** — where the graphic's form directly depicts its referent (lane stripes showing lane stripes, wing shapes in a poem about flight). This differs from abstract encoding and may warrant its own category.

Finally, Tufte's emphasis on "non-data-ink" and the goal of making every mark carry data suggests an efficiency intent distinct from mere density: **Eliminate Graphical Overhead** — removing elements that consume space/ink without conveying information.
