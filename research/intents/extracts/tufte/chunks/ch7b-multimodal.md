# Intent Extraction: Tufte Chapter 7-8 (Pages 153-164)

Source: Edward Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*
Pages: 153-164 (end of Chapter 7 "Multifunctioning Graphical Elements" and beginning of Chapter 8 "Data Density and Small Multiples")

## Extracted Communicative Intents

---

### 1. Puzzle Avoidance (Visual Immediacy)

**Name:** Puzzle Avoidance

**Definition:** The purpose of ensuring that graphics can be understood visually rather than requiring verbal decoding processes, so that interpretation becomes automatic and implicit.

**How Tufte articulates it:** "A sure sign of a puzzle is that the graphic must be interpreted through a verbal rather than a visual process." He contrasts puzzle graphics where "the viewers must run little phrases through their minds" with non-puzzle graphics where "the translation of visual to verbal is quickly learned, automatic, and implicit--so that the visual image flows right through the verbal decoder initially necessary to understand the graphic." He quotes Paul Valery: "Seeing is forgetting the name of the thing one sees." (p. 153)

**Specific examples:**
- The two-variable color map (16 shades across 3,056 counties) is identified as a puzzle graphic requiring mental phrases like "Now let's see, purple represents counties where there are both high levels of male cardiovascular disease mortality and 11.6 to 56.0 percent of households..."
- The crossed four-color grid encoding is called "a feat of technological virtuosity" but "surely a graphic experienced verbally, not visually"

**Framework category:** **Explanation** - relates to how understanding is achieved; puzzles obstruct comprehension

---

### 2. Natural Visual Ordering

**Name:** Natural Visual Ordering

**Definition:** The purpose of using visual encodings that have inherent perceptual hierarchy, allowing viewers to comprehend magnitude and sequence without learned conventions.

**How Tufte articulates it:** "Color often generates graphical puzzles. Despite our experiences with the spectrum in science textbooks and rainbows, the mind's eye does not readily give a visual ordering to colors." In contrast: "Because they do have a natural visual hierarchy, varying shades of gray show varying quantities better than color." (p. 154)

**Specific examples:**
- The "mountain-to-the-sea method" by Vauthier (white for snow/maximum, green for forests, yellow for plains, blue for sea/minimum) is described as "ingenious in idea but not very satisfactory in practice"
- Ten gray shades in the galaxies map "worked effectively"
- Red can reflect "higher levels than other colors, as in the hot spots of the cancer map"

**Framework category:** **Distributional** - encoding magnitude and spread through naturally ordered visual variables

---

### 3. Information Flow Organization

**Name:** Information Flow Organization

**Definition:** The purpose of using graphical architecture to promote ordered, sequenced, hierarchical flow of information from the graphic to the viewer's perception.

**How Tufte articulates it:** "Central to maintaining clarity in the face of the complex are graphical methods that organize and order the flow of graphical information presented to the eye." He asks: "How can graphical architecture promote the ordered, sequenced, hierarchical flow of information from the graphic to the mind's eye?" (p. 154)

**Specific examples:**
- The shades of gray "provide an easily comprehended order to the data measures"
- The population density map reveals layered information: corridors connecting urban complexes, effects of landforms, clusters along rivers, small towns along highways "linked like a string of pearls"

**Framework category:** **Relational** - establishes hierarchy and connections within data

---

### 4. Multiple Viewing Depths

**Name:** Multiple Viewing Depths

**Definition:** The purpose of designing graphics with multiple layers of information accessible at different scales of attention: overview from distance, detail up close, and implicit underlying structure.

**How Tufte articulates it:** "Graphics can be designed to have at least three viewing depths: (1) what is seen from a distance, an overall structure usually aggregated from an underlying microstructure; (2) what is seen up close and in detail, the fine structure of the data; and (3) what is seen implicitly, underlying the graphic--that which is behind the graphic." (p. 155)

**Specific examples:**
- The Census Bureau population density map where "each dot, except in urban centers, represents 500 people"
- The map reveals at different depths: urban corridors, landform effects (California's central valley, Appalachian valleys and ridges), river clusters, highway town strings
- "The map arrays, in effect, some 400,000 points on its implicit grid"

**Framework category:** **Aggregative** - enables overview-to-detail navigation of summarized information

---

### 5. Multiple Visual Angles

**Name:** Multiple Visual Angles

**Definition:** The purpose of organizing different aspects of multivariate data along separate, consistent viewing directions so each dimension maintains visual independence.

**How Tufte articulates it:** "Different visual angles for different aspects of the data also organize graphical information. Each separate line of sight should remain unchanging (preferably horizontal or vertical) as the eye watches for data variation off the flat of the line of sight. For multivariate work, several clear lines can be created." (p. 155)

**Specific examples:**
- Ayres' display of American divisions in France: "Three separate visual angles make the flow of information coherent: the profile of the horizon for the upward-moving time-series, the vertical for the composition of the bar, and the horizontal for each division's stay"
- "Thus while every drop of ink serves three different data display functions, each of the three comes to the eye with its own independence and integrity"

**Framework category:** **Comparative** - enables comparison along multiple consistent axes

---

### 6. Data Integration with Separation

**Name:** Data Integration with Separation

**Definition:** The purpose of showing information that is both connected through related content and separated through distinct visual paths, allowing multiple independent readings of the same graphic.

**How Tufte articulates it:** "The information shown is both integrated and separated: integrated through its connected content, separated in that the eye follows several different and uncluttered paths in looking over the data." (p. 159)

**Specific examples:**
- The table-graphic showing government tax collections (1970 vs 1979) for 15 countries
- Vertical reading: ranks countries by percentage, names spaced proportionally
- Horizontal reading: paired comparisons show change over years
- Diagonal reading: slopes compared across all lines, unusual slopes "stand out from the overall upward pattern"
- The arrow diagram shows three independent reading directions

**Framework category:** **Comparative** - enables multiple comparison modes within single display

---

### 7. Rank Change Communication

**Name:** Rank Change Communication

**Definition:** The purpose of showing how ordinal positions shift over time or between conditions, revealing both absolute values and relative movement.

**How Tufte articulates it:** Through the slope graph example: "The chart, when read vertically, ranks 15 countries by government tax collections in 1970 and again in 1979, with the names spaced in proportion to the percentages. Across the columns, the paired comparisons show how the numbers changed over the years." (p. 159)

**Specific examples:**
- Government receipts slope graph: Sweden moves from 46.9% (rank 1) to 57.4% (still rank 1)
- Britain moves from 40.7% (rank 4 in 1970) to 39.0% (rank 7 in 1979), visible through crossing lines
- The proportional spacing encodes both magnitude and rank simultaneously

**Framework category:** **Temporal** - shows change over time in both absolute and relative terms

---

### 8. Slope Deviation Detection

**Name:** Slope Deviation Detection

**Definition:** The purpose of enabling rapid identification of data elements that behave differently from the overall pattern through slope comparison.

**How Tufte articulates it:** "The slopes are also compared by reading down the collection of lines, and lines of unusual slope stand out from the overall upward pattern." (p. 159)

**Specific examples:**
- In the government receipts graphic, Britain's line notably diverges (moving down in rank)
- Most lines slope upward (increasing government receipts), making any flatter or descending line visually salient
- The parallel alignment of slopes creates a visual baseline for detecting anomalies

**Framework category:** **Comparative** - identifies deviation from expected pattern

---

### 9. Data Density Maximization

**Name:** Data Density Maximization

**Definition:** The purpose of using the eye's high resolution capability to present maximum information per unit area, respecting rather than underestimating visual perception capacity.

**How Tufte articulates it:** "Our eyes can make a remarkable number of distinctions within a small area." He provides the data density formula: "data density of a graphic = number of entries in data matrix / area of data graphic" (p. 161-162)

**Specific examples:**
- 625 points distinguishable per square inch using light grid lines
- 80 x 80 grid creates "25,281 distinctions" in one square inch
- Statistical Abstract typography: 185 characters per square inch actual density
- Maps with "distinctions at 0.1 mm mean 254 per inch"
- Cartographer quote: "conciseness is of the essence and high resolution graphics are a common denominator of cartography"

**Framework category:** **Aggregative** - efficient communication of summary information

---

### 10. Anti-Sparsity

**Name:** Anti-Sparsity

**Definition:** The purpose of avoiding graphics that waste visual space relative to information content, representing a failure to respect the viewer's perceptual capacity.

**How Tufte articulates it:** By calculating data densities that reveal underperformance: "this overwrought display (originally printed in five colors) presents a data matrix of four entries... The graph covers 26.5 square inches... resulting in a data density of .15 numbers per square inch... which is thin indeed." (p. 162)

**Specific examples:**
- The participation rate bar chart: 4 data entries in 26.5 square inches = 0.15 numbers/sq inch
- JASA style sheet example: "3.8 numbers per square inch" for 32 entries
- Contrast with New York weather history: "181 numbers per square inch"
- The extreme example uses five colors and 26.5 square inches for information that could fit in a sentence

**Framework category:** **Aggregative** - efficient use of display resources

---

### 11. Perceptual Capacity Respect

**Name:** Perceptual Capacity Respect

**Definition:** The purpose of designing graphics that take advantage of the eye's high resolution rather than assuming viewers need oversimplification.

**How Tufte articulates it:** "How many statistical graphics take advantage of the ability of the eye to detect large amounts of information in small spaces?" He demonstrates through measurement instrument examples: "scales of 20 increments to the centimeter and 50 to the inch" and reading fine print. (pp. 161-162)

**Specific examples:**
- Population density map effectively presenting 400,000 points
- Engineering/architectural instruments with fine scales
- Statistical Abstract readable at 12 lines per vertical inch
- Cartographic convention of 0.1mm resolution

**Framework category:** **Aggregative** - relates to efficient information presentation matching perceptual capability

---

### 12. Condensation of Sensations

**Name:** Condensation of Sensations

**Definition:** The purpose of achieving integrated, unified visual experience from complex data, analogous to artistic composition where elements cohere into a whole.

**How Tufte articulates it:** Through the Matisse quotation that opens the chapter break: "I want to reach that state of condensation of sensations which constitutes a picture." (p. 160)

**Specific examples:**
- The Matisse quote serves as epigraph connecting data graphics to visual art
- The population density map achieves this: individual dots aggregate into corridors, landforms, patterns
- The slope graph achieves this: individual country changes cohere into overall "upward pattern"

**Framework category:** **Aggregative** - the synthetic goal of summarization into coherent whole

---

### 13. Viewing Architecture Analysis

**Name:** Viewing Architecture Analysis

**Definition:** The purpose of systematically analyzing and designing the visual paths through which a viewer will navigate a graphic.

**How Tufte articulates it:** "Such an analysis of the viewing architecture of a graphic will help in creating and evaluating designs that organize complex information hierarchically." (p. 159)

**Specific examples:**
- The arrow diagram showing three reading directions for the table-graphic
- Decomposition of Ayres' divisions display into three visual angles
- Analysis of slope graph into vertical, horizontal, and comparative readings

**Framework category:** **Instructional** - a methodological intent about design evaluation

---

## Summary: Core Principles from This Section

This section establishes principles around two major themes:

**1. Anti-Puzzle Design:** Graphics should enable visual rather than verbal processing
- Natural visual ordering (gray over arbitrary color)
- Multiple viewing depths (overview, detail, implicit)
- Multiple visual angles (independent reading paths)
- Information flow organization

**2. Data Density:** Graphics should respect human perceptual capability
- High resolution capacity of the eye
- Data density as quality metric
- Anti-sparsity as design goal
- Condensation as aesthetic achievement

## Intent Source Categorization

### From Explicit Prose Statements
These intents were directly articulated by Tufte in text:

1. **Puzzle Avoidance** - "A sure sign of a puzzle is that the graphic must be interpreted through a verbal rather than a visual process"
2. **Natural Visual Ordering** - "the mind's eye does not readily give a visual ordering to colors... varying shades of gray show varying quantities better than color"
3. **Information Flow Organization** - "graphical methods that organize and order the flow of graphical information presented to the eye"
4. **Multiple Viewing Depths** - three depths explicitly enumerated
5. **Multiple Visual Angles** - "Each separate line of sight should remain unchanging"
6. **Data Integration with Separation** - "integrated through its connected content, separated in that the eye follows several different and uncluttered paths"
7. **Data Density Maximization** - formula provided and discussed
8. **Viewing Architecture Analysis** - "analysis of the viewing architecture of a graphic"

### From Visual Examples I Could See
These intents were demonstrated through the figures:

1. **Rank Change Communication** - The slope graph (p. 158) visually demonstrates countries' changing positions through connecting lines with proportionally spaced endpoints
2. **Slope Deviation Detection** - Looking at the slope graph, Britain's declining rank visually "stands out" from upward-trending majority
3. **Multiple Viewing Depths** - The Census population density map (pp. 156-157) visually demonstrates all three viewing depths: overall East-West density gradient at distance, individual dots and corridors up close, 400,000-point grid implicitly
4. **Data Integration with Separation** - The arrow diagram (p. 159) visually shows three independent reading paths
5. **Anti-Sparsity** - The participation rate bar chart (p. 163) visually demonstrates wasted space: enormous gray bars encoding just 3 numbers
6. **Perceptual Capacity Respect** - The steel products table excerpt (p. 161) visually demonstrates readable high-density tabular data

### Discoveries from Visual Examination Not Named in Text

1. **Geographic Pattern Revelation** - The population density map reveals landform influences (Appalachian ridges, California's Central Valley, river corridors) that Tufte mentions but doesn't theorize as a distinct communicative intent. The graphic's power lies in revealing geography's invisible hand on human settlement.

2. **Urban Corridor Communication** - The population density map shows "corridors connecting the major urban complexes" - a specific spatial relationship type (linear urban agglomeration) that emerges visually but isn't named as an intent category. This represents communicating linear connectivity distinct from point clusters.

3. **Proportional Label Spacing** - In the slope graph, country names are spaced vertically in proportion to their percentage values, not equally spaced. This embeds quantitative encoding in typography itself, using whitespace as data carrier. Mentioned but not theorized as a technique.

4. **Crossing-Line Salience** - In the slope graph, when lines cross (indicating rank reversal), the crossing creates visual salience not discussed in text. This is a discoverable pattern-detection mechanism inherent in the form.

5. **Color-Complexity Ceiling** - The two-variable color map (p. 153) demonstrates that there's a complexity ceiling where color encoding breaks down. Tufte doesn't quantify this (e.g., "no more than 4-5 distinguishable colors in sequence") but the visual makes the failure mode evident.

6. **Night-Sky Metaphor** - The population density map (dark background, white dots for population) visually evokes satellite imagery of city lights at night. This metaphorical resonance aids comprehension but isn't discussed - the encoding maps naturally to familiar imagery.
