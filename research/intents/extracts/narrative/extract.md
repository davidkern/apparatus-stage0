# Intents from Narrative Theory and Storytelling

This document explores communicative intents derived from narrative theory,
fiction writing craft, and oral storytelling traditions. These intents focus
on how stories communicate meaning through structure, character, tension,
and emotional arc.

## New Intent Category: Narrative

Narrative intents describe how stories communicate meaning through temporal
unfolding, character perspective, and emotional journey. Unlike Explanation
(building understanding of concepts) or Instruction (directing action),
Narrative intents guide the audience through an experience with its own
dramatic shape.

---

## Structural Intents: Story Arc

### Intent: Exposition

**Definition**: Establishing the baseline situation before disruption.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand the "normal
world" against which subsequent events will be measured. This creates the
contrast necessary for change to be meaningful.

**Examples**:
- "In the beginning, the codebase was clean and well-documented."
- Opening chapters that establish character routines and setting.
- The "Before" state in a case study.
- Establishing shots in film that show ordinary life.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Setting, Character Introduction, Status
- Contained by: Story, Narrative Arc

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to State/Status but with
temporal and narrative function—it establishes what will change.

---

### Intent: Inciting Incident

**Definition**: The event that disrupts equilibrium and launches the narrative.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to recognize the moment
when everything changes—the point of no return that initiates the main action.
Also called catalyst, story spark, or triggering event.

**Examples**:
- "Then the security breach was discovered."
- "On October 15, the production database crashed."
- The moment when a character receives a call, letter, or visitor that
  changes everything.
- The discovery of a bug that leads to a redesign.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Event (specific)
- Contained by: Story, Narrative Arc

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Distinct from Causation (which
explains why). Inciting Incident marks the moment, emphasizing disruption
rather than mechanism.

---

### Intent: Rising Action

**Definition**: Escalating complications that build toward crisis.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to experience mounting
stakes and increasing engagement. Each complication raises the difficulty
and deepens investment.

**Examples**:
- A series of attempted fixes that don't work, each revealing new problems.
- Obstacles that grow more challenging in sequence.
- The progressive uncovering of system complexity.
- Debugging narratives where each hypothesis leads to unexpected findings.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Complication, Attempt, Setback
- Contained by: Story, Narrative Arc

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Sequence but with
escalating tension rather than neutral progression.

---

### Intent: Climax

**Definition**: The point of maximum tension where the central conflict is
decided.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to experience the moment
of truth—the turning point after which resolution becomes possible.

**Examples**:
- "At 3 AM, the fix was finally deployed."
- The moment of decision in a technical debate.
- The final battle, confession, or revelation.
- Peak complexity before understanding emerges.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Decision, Revelation, Confrontation
- Contained by: Story, Narrative Arc

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: No direct analog. Related to
Emphasis/Warning in its intensity but distinct in structural function.

---

### Intent: Falling Action

**Definition**: The aftermath of the climax as consequences unfold.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to see how the decisive
moment plays out—what happens after the turning point.

**Examples**:
- "With the patch deployed, we watched the metrics stabilize."
- Characters dealing with the results of their decisions.
- The gradual restoration of order.
- Verification and validation after a fix.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Consequence, Aftermath
- Contained by: Story, Narrative Arc

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Causation (showing
effects) but framed as resolution rather than explanation.

---

### Intent: Denouement

**Definition**: The untying of remaining threads and establishment of new
equilibrium.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to experience closure—
understanding how the world has changed and what the new normal looks like.

**Examples**:
- "A month later, the refactored system had handled three traffic spikes
  without incident."
- The postmortem that establishes lessons learned.
- Characters shown in their new situations after the story.
- Epilogues that show long-term consequences.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Resolution, New Status
- Contained by: Story, Narrative Arc

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Summary and Status but
with closure function—it's not just what is, but what the journey means.

---

### Intent: Epilogue

**Definition**: Additional closure extending beyond the main narrative.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to see further
consequences, or to provide meta-commentary on the story just told.
Distinct from denouement in being optional and often temporally distant.

**Examples**:
- "Five years later, that debugging session had become team folklore."
- Flash-forwards showing long-term impact.
- "Where are they now?" sections.
- Sequel hooks in serialized narratives.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Flash-forward, Commentary
- Contained by: Story

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: No direct analog. A special form
of Transition/Farewell with narrative content.

## Perspective Intents: Voice and Point of View

### Intent: Narrative Voice

**Definition**: Establishing who is telling the story and from what
vantage point.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand through
whose consciousness or perspective events are filtered. This fundamentally
shapes interpretation.

**Sub-intents**:

**First-Person Witness**: Narrator is a character experiencing events.
- "I was the one who found the bug."
- Personal accounts of incidents.
- Creates intimacy and subjective reliability.

**Third-Person Limited**: External narrator with access to one character's
inner experience.
- "Sarah didn't realize the impact of her commit until hours later."
- Case studies focused on one team member's perspective.

**Third-Person Omniscient**: External narrator with full knowledge.
- "What neither team knew was that both were working on the same problem."
- Retrospective analyses with complete information.

**Containment**:
- Contains: (the narrative content filtered through this perspective)
- Contained by: Story

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Orthogonal to most categories—
Voice modifies how other intents are expressed rather than being content itself.

---

### Intent: Interior Monologue

**Definition**: Representing a character's internal thoughts and feelings.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to experience
interiority—the private mental life that contrasts with external action.

**Examples**:
- "I knew the fix was wrong even as I submitted it."
- Italicized internal thoughts in fiction.
- Stream-of-consciousness passages.
- The gap between what characters say and think.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Thought, Feeling, Doubt
- Contained by: Scene, Character (in narrative)

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: No direct analog. Related to
Explanation but subjective and character-bound rather than objective.

---

### Intent: Dialogue

**Definition**: Representing spoken exchange between characters.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to experience direct
interaction—what characters say to each other, revealing relationship,
conflict, and personality through speech.

**Sub-intents**:

**Direct Dialogue**: Exact words in quotation.
- "The database is down," she said.
- Creates immediacy and character voice.

**Indirect Dialogue**: Summary of what was said.
- She informed the team that the database had failed.
- Creates narrative flow and compression.

**Free Indirect Discourse**: Character voice blended with narrator.
- The database was down. Great. Just what she needed.
- Creates intimacy without quotation marks.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Statement, Question, Command (within dialogue)
- Contained by: Scene

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Distinct from Quotation (which
attributes source). Dialogue is dramatic and character-building.

## Tension Intents: Engagement and Suspense

### Intent: Hook

**Definition**: An opening that captures attention and creates curiosity.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to become immediately
invested, raising questions they want answered.

**Examples**:
- "We lost 47 million dollars in 23 minutes."
- Opening lines that pose mysteries or paradoxes.
- Cold opens that drop the audience into action.
- In medias res beginnings.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Question (implicit), Mystery, Paradox
- Contained by: Story, Section (at start)

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Attention-Capture
(from Aristotle research) but specifically narrative in function.

---

### Intent: Suspense

**Definition**: Creating uncertainty about outcome while maintaining
investment in resolution.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to experience anticipation—
to care about what happens next and be uncertain about the result.

**Examples**:
- "The deploy was scheduled for 3 PM. Everything depended on the next hour."
- Countdown scenarios with uncertain outcomes.
- Will-they-won't-they relationship tension.
- Delayed revelation of test results.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Uncertainty, Stakes, Deadline
- Contained by: Rising Action, Scene

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Uncertainty (Evidential)
but emotional rather than epistemic. Suspense is about caring, not just
not knowing.

---

### Intent: Cliffhanger

**Definition**: Suspending narrative at a moment of maximum uncertainty.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to experience interrupted
engagement—to be left wanting resolution, ensuring return.

**Examples**:
- Chapter endings on moments of danger or revelation.
- "To be continued..." after a crisis moment.
- Serial episode endings.
- The dramatic pause before a verdict.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Interrupted Action, Unresolved Question
- Contained by: Section (at end), Episode (at end)

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: No direct analog. A specific form
of Transition but with engagement rather than structural purpose.

---

### Intent: Foreshadowing

**Definition**: Planting hints about future events.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to receive advance
signals (conscious or unconscious) that prepare them for later developments,
creating coherence and retrospective satisfaction.

**Examples**:
- "The error logs would prove significant later."
- Chekhov's gun—details that will matter.
- Ominous warnings that prove accurate.
- Setup for future payoff.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Hint, Warning (subtle)
- Contained by: Scene, Exposition

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Warning but covert
rather than explicit. Foreshadowing doesn't announce itself.

---

## Temporal Intents: Narrative Time

### Intent: Flashback (Analepsis)

**Definition**: Interrupting present narrative to show past events.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand present
events through access to their history—showing how we got here.

**Examples**:
- "Three months earlier, the original architecture decision had been made."
- Memory sequences in film.
- Backstory revealed through scene rather than summary.
- The incident that explains current behavior.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Past Scene, Memory
- Contained by: Present Narrative

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Causation and Provenance
but dramatized rather than stated. Flashback shows; Causation tells.

---

### Intent: Flash-forward (Prolepsis)

**Definition**: Interrupting present narrative to show future events.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to know where things
are heading—creating dramatic irony or establishing stakes.

**Examples**:
- "In six months, this decision would cost the company millions."
- Opening with the ending, then going back to show how we arrived.
- Prophetic visions or premonitions.
- Frame stories where the narrator speaks from the future.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Future Scene, Outcome
- Contained by: Present Narrative

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Foreshadowing but
explicit rather than implicit. Flash-forward shows the future directly.

---

### Intent: In Medias Res

**Definition**: Beginning in the middle of action, with context supplied
later.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants immediate engagement, trusting the
audience to catch up as the narrative provides context retrospectively.

**Examples**:
- "The server was on fire. Three engineers hunched over terminals. Nobody
  knew who had pushed the change."
- Openings that drop the audience into crisis.
- Cold opens in television.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Present Crisis, Deferred Context
- Contained by: Story

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Hook but structural
rather than content-based. It's a strategy, not a moment.

---

## Character Intents: People in Stories

### Intent: Character Introduction

**Definition**: Presenting a character to the audience for the first time.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to form an initial
impression—to know who this person is and why they matter.

**Examples**:
- "Sarah had been at the company longer than anyone. She had survived
  three rewrites."
- First appearances that establish defining traits.
- "Enter MACBETH."
- Origin stories and biographical sketches.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Description, Trait, Role
- Contained by: Scene, Exposition

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Definition (naming
and explaining) but for people rather than concepts, with implicit
narrative relevance.

---

### Intent: Character Motivation

**Definition**: Revealing what a character wants and why.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand the
driving force behind character action—making behavior meaningful.

**Examples**:
- "She wanted the promotion, not for the money, but for the autonomy."
- Goal statements that explain subsequent choices.
- The "why" behind character decisions.
- Internal conflict between competing desires.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Goal, Want, Need
- Contained by: Character, Scene

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Causation but for
people rather than events. Also related to Interior Monologue when
internally expressed.

---

### Intent: Character Arc

**Definition**: The trajectory of change a character undergoes.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to see transformation—
how events and choices change a person over time.

**Examples**:
- "By the end of the year, she had become the kind of engineer she once
  criticized."
- Growth narratives in case studies.
- Before-and-after character states.
- The lessons a protagonist learns.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Starting State, Transformation, Ending State
- Contained by: Story

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Before/After
(Comparative) but specifically about people and implying growth or decline.

---

### Intent: Antagonism

**Definition**: Presenting opposition to the protagonist's goals.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand what
stands in the way—the forces creating conflict and obstacle.

**Examples**:
- "The legacy system resisted every change."
- Villain introductions and motivations.
- Competing teams or priorities.
- Technical debt as adversary.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Opposition, Obstacle, Threat
- Contained by: Story, Conflict

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: No direct analog. Creates the
tension that Emphasis/Warning responds to.

---

## Revelation Intents: Disclosure and Discovery

### Intent: Epiphany

**Definition**: A sudden realization that transforms understanding.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to experience a
moment of insight alongside the character—seeing something that was
always there but suddenly becomes clear.

**Examples**:
- "Suddenly, it clicked: the race condition wasn't in our code—it was
  in the library."
- Eureka moments in debugging narratives.
- Character realizations about themselves or others.
- The "aha" moment in problem-solving stories.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Insight, Realization
- Contained by: Scene, Character Arc

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Distinct from Definition
(which explains) and Claim+Evidence (which argues). Epiphany is
experiential—you feel the click.

---

### Intent: Anagnorisis

**Definition**: Recognition of true identity or situation, typically with
tragic or dramatic implications.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to experience the
moment when a character discovers something fundamental about who they
are or what they've done—often with irreversible consequences.

**Examples**:
- Oedipus discovering he killed his father.
- Realizing the bug you've been chasing was in your own code.
- The moment of understanding that changes everything.
- Discovery that a trusted system was compromised all along.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Identity, Truth, Consequence
- Contained by: Climax, Scene

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: A dramatic form of Epiphany with
darker implications. Related to Error acknowledgment but personal and
transformative.

---

### Intent: Mystery

**Definition**: Establishing a question that the narrative will work to
answer.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to experience
curiosity as a driving force—the desire to know that sustains engagement.

**Examples**:
- "Who pushed the breaking change?"
- The central question a detective novel explores.
- "How did the system stay up despite the configuration error?"
- Unsolved problems that frame investigation narratives.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Question, Unknown
- Contained by: Story, Investigation

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Uncertainty (Evidential)
but narrative rather than epistemic—the mystery is something to be solved
through story, not data.

---

### Intent: Revelation

**Definition**: The disclosure of previously hidden information.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to experience
discovery—learning something that recontextualizes what came before.

**Examples**:
- "What nobody knew was that the 'fix' had introduced three new bugs."
- Plot twists and surprise disclosures.
- Postmortem findings that change the story.
- The answer to the mystery.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Hidden Truth, New Information
- Contained by: Climax, Scene

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Explanation but with
dramatic timing. Revelation is about when information appears, not just
what it contains.

---

## Setting Intents: World and Place

### Intent: Scene-Setting

**Definition**: Establishing the spatial, temporal, and atmospheric
context for action.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to be located—to
know where and when events occur and what the environment feels like.

**Examples**:
- "It was 2 AM in the data center, server fans humming."
- Establishing shots in film.
- The description of a meeting room before the meeting begins.
- Atmospheric openings in fiction.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Location, Time, Atmosphere
- Contained by: Scene

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Status but spatial
and atmospheric rather than evaluative. Setting locates; Status assesses.

---

### Intent: World-Building

**Definition**: Constructing the larger context and rules within which
the narrative operates.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand the
world—its constraints, possibilities, and the logic that governs it.

**Examples**:
- Explaining how the deployment pipeline works before describing a
  deployment failure.
- Fantasy novels establishing magic systems.
- Technical architecture overviews that set up debugging narratives.
- The rules of the game before the game is played.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Rules, Constraints, Possibilities
- Contained by: Story, Exposition

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Definition and
Explanation but for systems rather than concepts. World-building creates
the possibility space.

---

## Frame Intents: Stories Within Stories

### Intent: Frame Narrative

**Definition**: A story that contains and contextualizes another story.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to experience layered
narrative—understanding that stories are being told within stories, with
the framing providing commentary or context.

**Examples**:
- "Let me tell you about the time we almost lost the production database."
- Scheherazade's frame in One Thousand and One Nights.
- A documentary about people telling stories.
- Nested retrospectives.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Inner Story, Narrator (in outer story)
- Contained by: Story

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: No direct analog. Related to
Quotation (embedding another's words) but for entire narratives.

---

### Intent: Embedded Narrative

**Definition**: A story told within the frame of an outer story.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to experience a story
within a story—often providing parallel, contrast, or symbolic commentary
on the outer narrative.

**Examples**:
- The player's tale within a larger chronicle.
- A character relating a past experience.
- The story Victor Frankenstein tells Walton.
- Case studies embedded in larger analyses.

**Containment**:
- Contains: (its own narrative structure)
- Contained by: Frame Narrative

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Example (illustrative
instance) but fully narrative rather than illustrative of a point.

---

## Oral Tradition Intents: Performance and Participation

### Intent: Call and Response

**Definition**: A structural pattern inviting audience participation at
predictable points.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author/performer wants the audience to become
co-creators—to participate actively rather than passively receive.

**Examples**:
- "When I say 'Cric!' you say 'Crac!'"
- Refrains in oral poetry that audiences join.
- Interactive storytelling prompts.
- Audience responses in religious or cultural rituals.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Prompt, Response (expected)
- Contained by: Performance, Oral Narrative

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Confirmation (request
for explicit response) but performative and rhythmic rather than
transactional.

---

### Intent: Formulaic Opening

**Definition**: A traditional phrase signaling that a story is beginning.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author/performer wants to shift the audience
into story-receiving mode—marking the boundary between ordinary discourse
and narrative space.

**Examples**:
- "Once upon a time..."
- "A long time ago, in a galaxy far, far away..."
- "Coyote was going there."
- Traditional tale openings in any culture.

**Containment**:
- Contains: (nothing—it's a boundary marker)
- Contained by: Story (at start)

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: A narrative form of Greeting/Marker.
It signals mode shift rather than just section boundary.

---

### Intent: Formulaic Closing

**Definition**: A traditional phrase signaling that a story has ended.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author/performer wants to mark the return from
narrative space to ordinary discourse—providing closure and frame exit.

**Examples**:
- "And they lived happily ever after."
- "And that's the story of..."
- "The end."
- Traditional tale closings in any culture.

**Containment**:
- Contains: (nothing—it's a boundary marker)
- Contained by: Story (at end)

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: A narrative form of Farewell/Marker.
Like Formulaic Opening, it signals mode rather than content.

---

### Intent: Repetition with Variation

**Definition**: A pattern repeated multiple times with progressive changes.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to experience rhythm
and anticipation—to recognize the pattern while awaiting the variation
that breaks or fulfills it.

**Examples**:
- The three little pigs: each encounter with the wolf follows and varies
  the pattern.
- Three attempts before success.
- Each retry with a different approach.
- "The rule of three" in comedy and storytelling.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Pattern, Variation
- Contained by: Story, Scene

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Sequence but with
emphasis on repetition and progressive change rather than linear progression.

---

## Symbolic Intents: Meaning Beyond the Literal

### Intent: Motif

**Definition**: A recurring element that accumulates meaning through
repetition.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to notice pattern and
draw meaning—elements that repeat until they become significant.

**Examples**:
- The mockingbird in To Kill a Mockingbird.
- Recurring imagery of water, light, or decay.
- A system metaphor used consistently throughout documentation.
- The green light in The Great Gatsby.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Recurring Element (image, phrase, idea)
- Contained by: Story

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: No direct analog. Related to
Pattern but semantic rather than structural.

---

### Intent: Symbolic Detail

**Definition**: A concrete element that carries abstract meaning.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to perceive meaning
beyond the literal—to understand that an object, action, or image stands
for something larger.

**Examples**:
- "The red test failure was more than a technical problem."
- Objects that represent themes or character states.
- Actions that carry symbolic weight.
- The literal and figurative levels of meaning.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Concrete Element, Abstract Meaning
- Contained by: Scene, Description

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Analogy (mapping
domains) but embedded in narrative rather than explicitly comparative.

---

## Summary: Proposed New Category

Based on narrative theory research, a **Narrative** category is warranted,
containing intents that:

1. Communicate through temporal unfolding (story arc)
2. Filter through consciousness (voice and perspective)
3. Create and resolve tension (suspense and revelation)
4. Represent people in motion (character)
5. Locate action in space/time (setting)
6. Layer stories within stories (frame)
7. Invite participation (oral tradition)
8. Carry meaning beyond the literal (symbolism)

### Proposed Category Structure

| Category | What it communicates | Sub-groups |
|----------|---------------------|------------|
| **Narrative** | Experience through story | Arc, Voice, Tension, Character, Setting, Frame, Performance, Symbol |

### Relationship to Existing Categories

| Existing Category | Narrative Relationship |
|------------------|------------------------|
| Temporal | Narrative uses time but for drama, not data |
| Comparative | Character Arc is comparative but personal |
| State | Exposition and Denouement are states but with narrative function |
| Transition | Formulaic openings/closings are transitions with mode-shift |
| Explanation | Revelation explains but with timing and drama |
| Evidential | Mystery involves uncertainty but as story engine |

### Intents Likely to Join Existing Categories

| Intent | Recommended Category | Notes |
|--------|---------------------|-------|
| Scene-Setting | State | A specialized Status for narrative contexts |
| Formulaic Opening/Closing | Transition | Narrative forms of Greeting/Farewell |

### Intents Requiring Narrative Category

- Exposition, Inciting Incident, Rising Action, Climax, Falling Action, Denouement, Epilogue (Arc)
- Narrative Voice, Interior Monologue, Dialogue (Voice)
- Hook, Suspense, Cliffhanger, Foreshadowing (Tension)
- Flashback, Flash-forward, In Medias Res (Temporal Manipulation)
- Character Introduction, Character Motivation, Character Arc, Antagonism (Character)
- World-Building (Setting)
- Frame Narrative, Embedded Narrative (Frame)
- Epiphany, Anagnorisis, Mystery, Revelation (Disclosure)
- Call and Response, Repetition with Variation (Oral Tradition)
- Motif, Symbolic Detail (Symbol)

## Open Questions

1. **Is Narrative a category or a mode?** Aristotle's Rhetoric distinguished
   rhetoric as the art of persuasion from its modes. Should Narrative be an
   orthogonal dimension (like Persuasive) rather than a content category?

2. **Relationship to Temporal category**: Many narrative intents involve time
   (flashback, flash-forward, sequence). Is Narrative a specialization of
   Temporal or a peer category that uses temporal forms differently?

3. **Voice as modifier**: Narrative Voice seems to modify how content is
   expressed rather than being content itself. Should perspective be a
   dimension rather than an intent?

4. **Oral tradition specificity**: Call and Response and Formulaic patterns
   may be too medium-specific (oral performance) for a medium-independent
   intent taxonomy. Should these be forms rather than intents?

5. **Fiction vs. non-fiction**: These intents derive from fiction and oral
   tradition. How do they apply to technical narratives (incident reports,
   case studies, debugging logs)? Are the forms transferable even when the
   content is factual?

6. **Emotional arc tracking**: Narrative theory emphasizes emotional arc as
   distinct from plot arc. Should there be explicit emotional-state intents
   (Dread, Hope, Relief, Satisfaction) or are these emergent from other
   intents?
