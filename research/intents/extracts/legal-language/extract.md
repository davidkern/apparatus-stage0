# Intents from Legal and Contractual Language

This document explores communicative intents derived from legal drafting conventions,
legislative language, contract interpretation principles, deontic logic, and legal
speech acts. Legal practice has systematized communicative purposes that everyday
language leaves ambiguous. The focus is on **general communicative intents** that
legal language has formalized—not legal-specific concepts, but universal patterns
of meaning that law has made precise.

## The Core Insight: Legal Language Makes Modality Explicit

Legal language has evolved to disambiguate what everyday communication leaves vague.
When someone says "you should do X," it could mean:
- "I recommend X" (advisory)
- "You are expected to do X" (normative expectation)
- "You are required to do X" (obligation)
- "You are allowed to do X" (permission)

Legal drafting forces precision. The distinction between **shall**, **must**, **may**,
**should**, and **will** represents centuries of refinement in expressing modality—
the relationship between an action and its normative status.

This is the primary contribution legal language makes to the intent catalog:
**deontic modality**—the system of obligation, permission, and prohibition.

## New Intent Category: Deontic

Deontic logic studies normative concepts: what is obligatory, permitted, or forbidden.
Legal language operationalizes this into a precise vocabulary. These intents describe
the normative relationship between an agent and an action.

### Intent: Obligation

**Definition**: Communicating that an action is required—the agent must perform it.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that a specific
action is mandatory. Failure to perform creates liability, breach, or violation.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **shall** (traditional): "The contractor shall deliver within 30 days"
- **must** (modern): "The user must provide valid credentials"
- **is required to**: "The applicant is required to submit form X"
- **will** (future obligation): "The licensee will maintain insurance"

**Informal Appearance**:
- "You need to..." (ambiguous—could be suggestion)
- "You should..." (even more ambiguous)
- "Make sure you..." (implied obligation)
- "Don't forget to..." (obligation framed as reminder)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Legal language distinguishes between:
- Strong obligation (shall/must): Legally enforceable requirement
- Expectation (should): Normative but not strictly enforceable
- Recommendation: Advisory only

**Containment**:
- Contains: Action description, Agent identification
- Contained by: Covenant, Condition, Rule

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Command (Instruction) but
distinct. Command is imperative—"do X." Obligation establishes a normative state—
"X is required of you." The distinction matters: a command can be ignored;
an obligation creates accountability.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Often in operative clauses, numbered lists of duties
- *Auditory*: Emphasized modal verb, serious tone
- *Visual*: Highlighted requirements, checkbox lists

---

### Intent: Permission

**Definition**: Communicating that an action is allowed—the agent may perform it.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that a specific
action is permitted. This grants discretion rather than imposing duty.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **may**: "The licensee may sublicense with written consent"
- **is permitted to**: "The user is permitted to copy for personal use"
- **is entitled to**: "The employee is entitled to 30 days notice"
- **has the right to**: "Each party has the right to terminate"

**Informal Appearance**:
- "You can..." (ambiguous—ability vs permission)
- "Feel free to..." (permission with encouragement)
- "It's okay to..." (permission with reassurance)
- "You're allowed to..." (explicit permission)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Legal language distinguishes:
- Permission (may): Authorized to do
- Ability (can): Capable of doing
- Recommendation (should): Advised to do

"Can you do X?" asks about ability or permission—legal language never uses "can"
for permission because of this ambiguity.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Action description, Agent identification, Conditions (optional)
- Contained by: License, Grant, Rights clause

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Suggestion (Instruction)
but different direction. Suggestion advises action; Permission authorizes it.
Permission is an enablement, not a nudge.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Often in rights sections, positive grants
- *Auditory*: Lighter tone than obligation, emphasis on "may"
- *Visual*: Distinct from requirements, often in separate section

---

### Intent: Prohibition

**Definition**: Communicating that an action is forbidden—the agent must not perform it.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that a specific
action is not permitted. Performance constitutes violation.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **shall not**: "The licensee shall not disclose confidential information"
- **may not**: "Users may not reverse engineer the software"
- **must not**: "The contractor must not subcontract without approval"
- **is prohibited from**: "Employees are prohibited from..."

**Informal Appearance**:
- "Don't..." (direct prohibition)
- "You can't..." (ambiguous—inability vs prohibition)
- "Never..." (strong informal prohibition)
- "Avoid..." (weak prohibition or recommendation)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Legal language distinguishes:
- Prohibition (shall not/may not): Forbidden action
- Inability (cannot): Impossible action
- Disrecommendation (should not): Advised against but not forbidden

Note: "May not" in legal usage means "is not permitted to"—the denial of
permission. In casual usage, "may not" can mean "might not" (uncertainty).

**Containment**:
- Contains: Action description, Agent identification
- Contained by: Restriction, Limitation, Negative covenant

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Inverse of Permission. Related to
Warning (Emphasis) when consequences are stated, but Warning is about outcomes
while Prohibition is about rules.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Often highlighted, restrictive covenants section
- *Auditory*: Serious tone, emphasis on "not"
- *Visual*: Warning styling, often with negative indicators

---

### Intent: Entitlement

**Definition**: Communicating that an agent has a right that others must respect.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that a specified
party has a claim that creates corresponding duties in others.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **is entitled to**: "The employee is entitled to severance pay"
- **has the right to**: "The buyer has the right to inspect"
- **shall receive**: "The consultant shall receive payment within 30 days"

**Informal Appearance**:
- "You get..." (casual entitlement)
- "You deserve..." (moral entitlement)
- "They owe you..." (entitlement from other's perspective)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Entitlement differs from Permission:
- Permission: "You may do X" (you are authorized)
- Entitlement: "You are entitled to X" (others must provide/allow)

Entitlements create duties in counterparties; permissions merely remove barriers.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Right description, Beneficiary identification
- Contained by: Rights clause, Benefits section

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: No direct analog. Related to Permission
but creates corresponding obligation in others. An entitlement is bilateral;
permission is unilateral.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Benefits sections, positive rights statements
- *Auditory*: Affirmative tone, emphasis on "entitled"
- *Visual*: Distinct formatting for benefits/rights

## New Intent Category: Conditionality

Legal language has formalized patterns for expressing conditions, exceptions,
and contingencies. These intents describe relationships between triggering
events and consequences.

### Intent: Condition-Precedent

**Definition**: Communicating that an obligation or right only arises after
a specified event occurs.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that something
becomes operative only when a triggering condition is satisfied.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **upon**: "Upon completion, payment shall be due"
- **if and when**: "If and when approval is obtained..."
- **subject to**: "Subject to regulatory approval, the merger..."
- **provided that** (conditional): "Payment due, provided that work is complete"

**Informal Appearance**:
- "Once you..." (triggering event)
- "After..." (temporal condition)
- "When..." (event-triggered)
- "As soon as..." (immediacy after condition)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Legal language distinguishes:
- Condition-Precedent: Event must occur *before* obligation arises
- Condition-Subsequent: Event occurring *after* can extinguish obligation
- Mere timing: Event may or may not affect obligation

**Containment**:
- Contains: Trigger description, Consequence description
- Contained by: Operative provisions, Rights/obligations clauses

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Temporal intents (Sequence)
but focused on normative activation rather than temporal ordering. The condition
isn't just "before"; it's "required before."

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: "If-then" structure, often in numbered provisions
- *Auditory*: Clear pause between condition and consequence
- *Visual*: Flow diagrams, conditional branching

---

### Intent: Condition-Subsequent

**Definition**: Communicating that an existing obligation or right terminates
upon occurrence of a specified event.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that something
currently operative will cease if a triggering condition occurs.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **until**: "License continues until termination"
- **unless and until**: "Obligation continues unless and until..."
- **terminates upon**: "Agreement terminates upon bankruptcy"
- **subject to termination if**: "Rights subject to termination if..."

**Informal Appearance**:
- "Until..." (ending condition)
- "Unless..." (exception that ends state)
- "This stops when..." (termination trigger)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Condition-subsequent differs from condition-precedent
in directionality:
- Precedent: Nothing until X happens
- Subsequent: Everything until X happens

**Containment**:
- Contains: Termination trigger, Current state description
- Contained by: Term provisions, Termination clauses

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Duration (Temporal) but
focused on contingent termination rather than fixed time.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Termination sections, exception clauses
- *Auditory*: Emphasis on termination trigger
- *Visual*: Exit points in flow diagrams

---

### Intent: Exception

**Definition**: Communicating that a general rule does not apply in a
specified circumstance.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that while
a rule generally applies, it has carve-outs for specific situations.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **except**: "All information is confidential except..."
- **unless**: "Payment due within 30 days unless..."
- **other than**: "Applies to all products other than..."
- **save for**: "Binding on all parties save for..."
- **notwithstanding**: "Notwithstanding the foregoing..." (introduces override)

**Informal Appearance**:
- "But not..." (exclusion)
- "Except when..." (situational exception)
- "Unless..." (conditional exception)
- "This doesn't apply to..." (explicit exception)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Legal language distinguishes:
- Exception: Removes specific cases from general rule
- Condition: Makes rule contingent on something
- Limitation: Reduces scope of rule

Exceptions cancel the default; conditions delay or contingentize it.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Exception description, Scope of exception
- Contained by: General rules, Operative provisions

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: No direct analog. Related to Deviation
(Comparative) but normative rather than descriptive. Exceptions define the
boundary of a rule's application.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Parenthetical clauses, "except" phrases, separate subsections
- *Auditory*: Distinctly marked qualification, often with pause
- *Visual*: Indented or separated exception text

---

### Intent: Override

**Definition**: Communicating that a provision takes precedence over conflicting
provisions.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that when
conflict exists, this provision wins.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **notwithstanding**: "Notwithstanding any other provision..."
- **shall prevail**: "In case of conflict, this section shall prevail"
- **takes precedence**: "This agreement takes precedence over..."
- **supersedes**: "This agreement supersedes all prior agreements"

**Informal Appearance**:
- "Even though..." (acknowledges conflict)
- "Regardless of..." (overrides other considerations)
- "Despite..." (acknowledges but overrides)
- "This trumps..." (casual override)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Override establishes hierarchy when rules conflict.
Without explicit override language, conflicts create ambiguity. Legal
language makes precedence explicit.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Prevailing provision reference, Subordinated provisions
- Contained by: Entire agreement clauses, Interpretation provisions

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: No direct analog. This is
meta-communicative—it's about how to interpret other communications
when they conflict.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Often in "General Provisions" section
- *Auditory*: Emphasis on precedence terms
- *Visual*: Hierarchy diagrams for document precedence

---

### Intent: Preservation

**Definition**: Communicating that remaining provisions survive despite
invalidity of one.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that
partial failure doesn't cause total failure. This is the severability intent.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **severability**: "If any provision is held invalid, the remainder..."
- **savings clause**: Preserves validity of remaining terms
- **shall remain in effect**: "Other provisions shall remain in effect"

**Informal Appearance**:
- "The rest still applies..."
- "This doesn't affect..."
- "Even if X fails, Y still works..."

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Without preservation intent, an invalid provision
might void an entire agreement. Severability makes partial validity explicit.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Invalid provision acknowledgment, Preserved provisions
- Contained by: General provisions, Boilerplate

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: No direct analog. This is defensive
communication about the document's integrity.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Standard boilerplate clause
- *Auditory*: Formal, reassuring tone
- *Visual*: Often in fine print but legally significant

## New Intent Category: Commitment

Legal language formalizes different types of commitments about facts and future
actions. This category captures the distinction between statements about present
facts, promises about future conduct, and guarantees about truth.

### Intent: Representation

**Definition**: Communicating a statement of present or past fact made to
induce reliance.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to accept a factual
assertion as true and rely upon it.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **represents that**: "The seller represents that it owns the property"
- **states and represents**: Formal assertion of fact
- **to the best of [party's] knowledge**: Qualified representation

**Informal Appearance**:
- "I'm telling you that..." (assertion)
- "The fact is..." (factual claim)
- "As far as I know..." (qualified assertion)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Representation differs from mere assertion:
- Representation: Factual statement inducing reliance, with remedies for falsity
- Assertion: General statement without explicit reliance context

False representation can void a contract; false casual assertion typically cannot.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Factual assertion, Knowledge qualifier (optional)
- Contained by: Representations section, Preamble

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Claim+Evidence (Evidential)
but about inducement and reliance rather than argumentation. Representations
create liability; claims seek persuasion.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Representations section, often in schedules
- *Auditory*: Formal, declarative tone
- *Visual*: Distinct section formatting

---

### Intent: Warranty

**Definition**: Communicating a promise that an assertion is true, with implied
commitment to make good if false.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that the
author stands behind a statement and will bear consequences if it's wrong.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **warrants that**: "The vendor warrants that the product is free of defects"
- **represents and warrants**: Combined assertion and guarantee
- **guarantees**: "The manufacturer guarantees performance"

**Informal Appearance**:
- "I guarantee..." (strong promise)
- "I promise it's..." (assurance of truth)
- "You can count on..." (reliability claim)
- "I stand behind..." (commitment to assertion)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Warranty differs from representation:
- Representation: Statement of fact (past/present)
- Warranty: Promise that statement is true + commitment to remedy

Breach of warranty entitles party to benefit of bargain; misrepresentation
may allow contract avoidance.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Warranted fact, Scope limitations
- Contained by: Warranties section

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Confidence (Evidential)
in that it expresses certainty, but Warranty creates liability. The author
isn't just confident—they're accountable.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Warranties section, often extensive in commercial contracts
- *Auditory*: Emphatic, committed tone
- *Visual*: Highlighted guarantees, warranty badges

---

### Intent: Covenant

**Definition**: Communicating a promise of future action or inaction.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that the
author commits to doing (or not doing) something in the future.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **covenants to**: "The borrower covenants to maintain insurance"
- **agrees to**: "The licensee agrees to pay royalties quarterly"
- **undertakes to**: "The contractor undertakes to complete by..."
- **shall** (as promise): "The party shall indemnify..."

**Informal Appearance**:
- "I will..." (future commitment)
- "I promise to..." (explicit promise)
- "I commit to..." (commitment declaration)
- "Count on me to..." (reliability promise)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Covenant differs from representation/warranty:
- Representation: About present/past facts
- Warranty: Guarantee of truth
- Covenant: Promise of future conduct

The temporal direction differs: representations look backward, covenants
look forward.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Promised action, Timeframe (often)
- Contained by: Covenants section, Obligations

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Commissive (Conversation
Analysis discovery) but more formal. Covenants are legally binding promises;
commissives are speech acts that may or may not create legal obligation.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Covenants section, obligations lists
- *Auditory*: Forward-looking, committed tone
- *Visual*: Action items, commitment trackers

## New Intent Category: Scope

Legal language has developed precise vocabulary for delimiting what is and
isn't covered by a provision. These intents communicate boundaries.

### Intent: Limitation

**Definition**: Communicating the boundaries within which something applies.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand the extent
and bounds of a right, obligation, or provision.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **limited to**: "License is limited to personal use"
- **solely for**: "Information solely for evaluation purposes"
- **only**: "Applies only to registered users"
- **to the extent**: "Liable only to the extent of..."

**Informal Appearance**:
- "Just for..." (purpose limitation)
- "Only covers..." (scope boundary)
- "Doesn't include..." (exclusion)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Limitation differs from exception:
- Limitation: Defines the scope from the start
- Exception: Carves out from otherwise broader scope

Limitations say "this is how big it is"; exceptions say "but not this part."

**Containment**:
- Contains: Scope boundary, Subject of limitation
- Contained by: Grants, Rights, Licenses

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Definition (Explanation)
but focused on boundaries rather than meaning. Limitations constrain; definitions
explain.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Often parenthetical or in same sentence as grant
- *Auditory*: Qualifying tone, emphasis on boundaries
- *Visual*: Boundary markers, scope diagrams

---

### Intent: Definition

**Definition**: Communicating the precise meaning of a term as used in context.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to use a term with a
specific meaning throughout, eliminating ambiguity.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **"Term" means**: "Confidential Information" means..."
- **as defined herein**: "As used in this Agreement..."
- **shall have the meaning**: "The following terms shall have the meanings..."

**Informal Appearance**:
- "By X, I mean..." (explicit definition)
- "X is..." (implicit definition)
- "When I say X..." (contextual definition)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Legal definitions are stipulative—they create meaning
for the document's purposes regardless of ordinary usage. This eliminates
disputes over word meaning.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Term, Definition
- Contained by: Definitions section, Recitals

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: The framework already has Definition
(Explanation). Legal usage is more stipulative and binding—the definition
isn't just explaining meaning but establishing it authoritatively.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Definitions section, capitalized defined terms
- *Auditory*: Clear term-definition pairing
- *Visual*: Glossary format, term highlighting

---

### Intent: Enumeration

**Definition**: Communicating a list where the list itself has semantic
significance (exhaustive vs. illustrative).

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand whether
a list is complete or exemplary.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **including but not limited to**: List is illustrative, not exhaustive
- **including without limitation**: Same as above
- **means the following**: List is exhaustive
- **limited to**: List is exhaustive
- **such as**: List is illustrative

**Informal Appearance**:
- "Like..." (illustrative)
- "For example..." (illustrative)
- "These are..." (ambiguous—could be exhaustive or illustrative)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Is a list all there is, or just examples?
- Exhaustive: Only listed items qualify
- Illustrative: Listed items are examples; others may qualify

This distinction determines whether unlisted items are included.

**Containment**:
- Contains: List items, List type marker
- Contained by: Definitions, Obligations, Conditions

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Inventory (State) and
Example (Explanation) but focused on the meta-question of completeness.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Numbered lists with framing language
- *Auditory*: Explicit marker of list type
- *Visual*: List with "and more" or closed boundary

## New Intent Category: Effort-Level

Legal language has developed precise vocabulary for different levels of
effort required in fulfilling obligations. These intents communicate
the standard of performance.

### Intent: Best-Efforts

**Definition**: Communicating that maximum reasonable exertion is required.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that
all reasonable steps must be taken, leaving no stone unturned.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **best efforts**: "Shall use best efforts to obtain approval"
- **all reasonable efforts**: Variant formulation
- **best endeavours** (UK): British equivalent

**Informal Appearance**:
- "Do everything you can..." (strong effort expectation)
- "Really try to..." (strong encouragement)
- "Make it happen..." (high-effort imperative)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: "Best efforts" is the highest standard—short of
acting against one's own interests. Below it are gradations.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Effort standard, Target outcome
- Contained by: Obligation, Covenant

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Modifies Obligation—it's not just
"do X" but "do X with maximum reasonable effort." This is a dimension
that cross-cuts obligations.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Precedes obligation statement
- *Auditory*: Emphasis on effort standard
- *Visual*: Could be styled as high-priority commitment

---

### Intent: Reasonable-Efforts

**Definition**: Communicating that diligent effort with consideration of
one's own interests is required.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that
effort is required but balanced against other legitimate concerns.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **reasonable efforts**: "Shall use reasonable efforts"
- **commercially reasonable efforts**: Business-context variant
- **reasonable endeavours** (UK): British equivalent

**Informal Appearance**:
- "Try to..." (moderate effort expectation)
- "See what you can do..." (effort without absolute commitment)
- "Do your best within reason..." (balanced effort)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Reasonable efforts allows party to consider costs,
feasibility, and competing priorities. It's effort "within reason."

**Containment**:
- Contains: Effort standard, Target outcome, Balancing factors
- Contained by: Obligation, Covenant

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Moderate version of Best-Efforts.
These form a spectrum of effort-level intents.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Standard effort qualifier
- *Auditory*: Moderate emphasis
- *Visual*: Standard commitment level

---

### Intent: Good-Faith

**Definition**: Communicating that honest and fair dealing is required.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that
while specific actions aren't mandated, honesty and fairness are.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **good faith**: "Act in good faith"
- **good faith and fair dealing**: Fuller formulation
- **bona fide**: Latin equivalent

**Informal Appearance**:
- "Be honest about..." (honesty expectation)
- "Deal fairly..." (fairness expectation)
- "No funny business..." (informal good faith)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Good faith is the baseline—implied in most contracts.
It focuses on manner rather than result: don't deceive, don't obstruct,
don't act opportunistically.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Manner standard
- Contained by: Performance obligations, Conduct requirements

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Face-Work (Conversation
Analysis) in social dimension, but Good-Faith is legal—breach has remedies.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Often implied, sometimes explicit in interpretation clauses
- *Auditory*: Tone of trust and expectation
- *Visual*: Trust indicators

## New Intent Category: Liability

Legal language precisely allocates responsibility for harm. These intents
communicate who bears risk and responsibility.

### Intent: Indemnification

**Definition**: Communicating that one party will compensate another for
specified losses.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that
if certain harms occur, a specified party will make the other whole.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **shall indemnify**: "Seller shall indemnify Buyer against..."
- **hold harmless**: "Shall hold harmless from and against..."
- **defend, indemnify and hold harmless**: Full trilogy

**Informal Appearance**:
- "I'll cover you if..." (protection promise)
- "You won't have to pay..." (loss protection)
- "I've got you covered..." (informal indemnity)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Indemnification allocates risk explicitly:
- Who pays for what losses
- When obligation triggers
- Scope of covered losses

Without indemnification, loss allocation follows default rules that may
not match parties' expectations.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Covered losses, Indemnifying party, Indemnified party
- Contained by: Risk allocation section

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Covenant (commitment to
future action) but specific to loss compensation. It's a specialized promise.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Indemnification section, often detailed
- *Auditory*: Serious, protective tone
- *Visual*: Risk allocation diagrams

---

### Intent: Disclaimer

**Definition**: Communicating that certain warranties or representations
are NOT being made.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that
they should not rely on unstated assurances.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **as is**: "Sold as is, without warranty"
- **disclaims all warranties**: "Seller disclaims all warranties"
- **makes no representation**: "Party makes no representation regarding..."
- **without warranty of merchantability**: Specific disclaimer

**Informal Appearance**:
- "No guarantees..." (informal disclaimer)
- "I can't promise..." (disclaimer of commitment)
- "Use at your own risk..." (risk shifting)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Without disclaimer, implied warranties may exist.
Disclaimer makes explicit what is NOT warranted, preventing false expectations.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Disclaimed warranties, Disclaimed representations
- Contained by: Warranty section, Risk allocation

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Inverse of Warranty. Also related
to Disclaimer (Conversation Analysis—prospective face protection) but legal
Disclaimer is about liability, not face.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Often in caps or bold (to be conspicuous)
- *Auditory*: Clear, formal tone
- *Visual*: Warning styling, distinct formatting

---

### Intent: Exclusion

**Definition**: Communicating that certain types of damages or liability
are not recoverable.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand the
limits of available remedies.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **in no event shall**: "In no event shall either party be liable for..."
- **shall not be liable for**: Liability exclusion
- **consequential damages**: Often excluded category
- **cap on liability**: Monetary limit on exposure

**Informal Appearance**:
- "I'm not responsible for..." (informal exclusion)
- "That's not on me..." (liability rejection)
- "Maximum I'll pay is..." (cap communication)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: Without exclusion, full damages may be available.
Exclusion limits exposure and sets expectations about remedies.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Excluded damages, Liability caps
- Contained by: Limitation of liability section

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Limitation (Scope) but
specific to liability. It's about what can't be recovered.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Often in caps, standalone section
- *Auditory*: Serious, definitive tone
- *Visual*: Clear boundary styling

## New Intent Category: Temporal-Legal

Legal language has developed precise vocabulary for when obligations arise,
persist, and terminate. These extend the framework's Temporal category
with normative dimensions.

### Intent: Effective-Date

**Definition**: Communicating when provisions become operative.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to know when obligations
and rights "turn on."

**Legal Formalization**:
- **effective as of**: "Effective as of January 1, 2024"
- **shall take effect**: "This agreement shall take effect upon..."
- **from and after**: "From and after the Closing Date"

**Informal Appearance**:
- "Starting..." (commencement)
- "Beginning on..." (start marker)
- "As of..." (effective date marker)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: When exactly does something start? Effective-Date
makes the activation moment precise.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Date/event specification
- Contained by: Term provisions

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Timeline (Temporal) but
focused on normative activation rather than event ordering.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Opening provisions, often highlighted
- *Auditory*: Clear date announcement
- *Visual*: Calendar marker, timeline highlight

---

### Intent: Duration

**Definition**: Communicating how long something persists.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand the
temporal extent of a right or obligation.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **for a period of**: "For a period of five years"
- **term**: "The term of this Agreement shall be..."
- **during**: "During the term of this Agreement"
- **perpetual**: "In perpetuity"

**Informal Appearance**:
- "For..." (time period)
- "Until..." (endpoint)
- "Forever..." (perpetuity)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: How long does this last? Duration makes temporal
extent explicit.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Time period specification
- Contained by: Term provisions, License grants

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: The framework has Duration (Temporal).
Legal usage adds precision and binding force to duration statements.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Term section, often early in document
- *Auditory*: Clear period statement
- *Visual*: Timeline, calendar range

---

### Intent: Survival

**Definition**: Communicating that certain provisions persist beyond
termination of the main agreement.

**Semantic Purpose**: The author wants the audience to understand that
termination doesn't end everything—some provisions outlast it.

**Legal Formalization**:
- **shall survive**: "This section shall survive termination"
- **surviving provisions**: Section listing what persists
- **notwithstanding termination**: "Notwithstanding termination..."

**Informal Appearance**:
- "Even after it's over..." (post-termination persistence)
- "This still applies after..." (continuing effect)

**Ambiguity Resolved**: When agreement ends, what persists? Without survival
language, termination might end all obligations. Survival makes persistence
explicit.

**Containment**:
- Contains: Surviving provisions list
- Contained by: General provisions, Termination section

**Relationship to Existing Categories**: Related to Duration but specifically
about post-termination continuation. It's duration beyond normal term.

**Forms**:
- *Typographic*: Survival section, often in boilerplate
- *Auditory*: Emphasis on "survive"
- *Visual*: Persistent obligations highlighted

### Unique Contributions

Legal language contributes intents not systematically addressed elsewhere:

1. **Deontic precision**: The shall/may/must vocabulary systematizes
   normative modality that other domains leave vague. Technical writing
   uses RFC 2119 (MUST/SHOULD/MAY) precisely because legal language
   demonstrated the need.

2. **Conditional structure**: Legal language distinguishes conditions
   precedent from conditions subsequent, exceptions from limitations.
   These are temporal-normative hybrids not captured by pure temporal
   or pure normative categories.

3. **Commitment types**: The representation/warranty/covenant distinction
   creates a taxonomy of promises based on temporal reference (past/present
   fact vs. future conduct) and remedial consequences.

4. **Effort gradations**: Best efforts → reasonable efforts → good faith
   creates a spectrum of performance standards applicable whenever
   obligations involve discretionary effort.

5. **Explicit scope control**: Limitation, enumeration (exhaustive vs.
   illustrative), and exception language provide tools for precise
   scope communication.

## Cross-Cutting Observations

### 1. Modality as a Dimension

Legal language suggests that **deontic modality** (obligation/permission/
prohibition) may be orthogonal to content categories. Any action can be:
- Obligatory (shall)
- Permitted (may)
- Forbidden (shall not)
- Entitled (is entitled to)

This suggests modality might be a dimension that applies to Instruction
intents, making Command + Obligation different from Command + Permission
(which is really just Suggestion + Authorization).

### 2. Temporal-Normative Hybrids

Condition-Precedent and Condition-Subsequent are neither purely temporal
nor purely normative—they sit at the intersection. "After X" (temporal)
becomes "only after X" (condition-precedent: normative activation tied
to temporal event). This suggests the Temporal category needs expansion
to handle normative relationships to time.

### 3. Meta-Communication About Document Structure

Override, Preservation, and Survival are meta-communicative—they're not
about content but about how to interpret and apply the document itself.
This parallels the "interactional" intents from Conversation Analysis,
which manage the communication channel rather than conveying content.

### 4. Gradations and Spectrums

Legal language reveals that many intents exist on gradations:
- Obligation strength: Must → Shall → Should → May
- Effort level: Best → Reasonable → Good faith
- Assertion type: Warrants → Represents → States

These gradations suggest that some intent categories have internal
hierarchies of strength or commitment level.

### 5. Reliance and Accountability

Legal language makes explicit what's implicit in much communication:
that statements create reliance and speakers are accountable for what
they say. Representation and Warranty formalize the social contract
of communication—you can't just say things without consequence.

## Open Questions

1. **Is Deontic a category or a dimension?** Permission and Obligation
   feel like they could be modifiers on other intents (a Permission to
   do a Step, an Obligation to follow a Procedure) rather than
   standalone intents.

2. **How do legal Conditions relate to programming conditionals?**
   Technical documentation has if-then logic. Are legal conditions
   the same intent with different stakes?

3. **Should Effort-Level be a dimension?** Best-Efforts and
   Reasonable-Efforts seem to modify Obligation rather than being
   standalone intents. They answer "how much?" not "what?"

4. **Where does interpretation guidance fit?** Legal language includes
   meta-instructions like "shall be construed liberally" or "strict
   interpretation." These are about reading, not content.

5. **Can non-legal contexts use legal precision?** RFC 2119 suggests
   technical writing benefits from deontic precision. Should the
   framework recommend legal-style modality for all instruction intents?

6. **How do implicit vs explicit warrants differ?** Informal
   communication often implies warranties that legal language would
   make explicit. Is the difference in formality or meaning?

## Implications for the Framework

Legal language analysis suggests several expansions:

### 1. Add Deontic Category or Dimension

The shall/may/must distinction is fundamental. Either:
- Create a Deontic category with Obligation, Permission, Prohibition,
  Entitlement as intents, OR
- Treat modality as a dimension that qualifies Instruction intents

### 2. Expand Temporal Category

Add normative-temporal intents:
- Condition-Precedent (activation upon event)
- Condition-Subsequent (termination upon event)
- Effective-Date (when provisions activate)
- Survival (persistence beyond termination)

### 3. Add Commitment Types

Distinguish types of assertions by accountability:
- Representation (factual statement, reliance-inducing)
- Warranty (guarantee of truth)
- Covenant (promise of future conduct)

### 4. Add Scope Precision Intents

- Limitation (boundaries)
- Exception (carve-outs)
- Enumeration with list-type (exhaustive/illustrative)

### 5. Add Effort-Level as Dimension

Obligations may have effort qualifications:
- Best-Efforts
- Reasonable-Efforts
- Good-Faith

### 6. Add Meta-Document Intents

Intents about document interpretation:
- Override (precedence among provisions)
- Preservation (severability)
- Survival (post-termination persistence)

These would join a meta-communicative category with Interactional intents
from Conversation Analysis.

### 7. Consider RFC 2119 Integration

Technical writing's RFC 2119 keywords (MUST, SHOULD, MAY, etc.) operationalize
legal modality for specifications. The framework could recommend similar
precision for all instruction-category intents.
